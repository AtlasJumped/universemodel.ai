Human trials

The first human trial: 1,000 veterans, one year, one campus

This page sets out, from start to finish, how we would test the Holistic Model of Reality (HMR) in people for the first time: safely, with measured results, and in the open. It is a design, not a running study. Nothing here has been funded, approved, or started.

Every statement on this page carries one of four labels: established science or law with a source we opened, HMR's view, our estimate with the method shown, or open. A trial like this cannot begin without a principal investigator and the approval of an Institutional Review Board. We have written the design so that both can see the whole of it.

1. Summary

What we would do, and what it would show

One thousand United States veterans live for one year on a purpose-built campus beside a military base in Southern California. Housing, food, and clothing are provided. Each day follows the HMR Fascia Health Protocol: short showers, long immersion, stretching, and movement, inside a community built on HMR's values. Outcomes are measured at the start, during, and at the end.

Avenue one

Mathematics and physics

HMR is trying to show, in mathematics and physics, that a universe can follow step by step from Universal Intelligence. That work is on the Math and Physics pages, with every open step marked.

Avenue two

Fascia in the human body

HMR also holds that the body is a tensegrity and that caring for the fascia web changes health in ways that can be measured. That is what this trial tests.

HMR's viewThe two avenues come from one model, and they are separate tests. We say this plainly: a breakthrough in either would earn a hearing for the other. A result in fascia would not prove the mathematics, and a result in mathematics would not prove the fascia claims. Each has to stand on its own evidence.

2. Why veterans

Why the first thousand should be veterans

Veterans carry the kind of damage this protocol is meant to address, in very large numbers. They know how to live by a mission, in close quarters, through hard days. The country owes them.

2,312,985

Establishedservice-connected disabilities for limited flexion of the knee, the second most common of all, after tinnitus (VA Annual Benefits Report, fiscal year 2025).

17.8 million

Establishedmusculoskeletal disabilities on the VA's rolls, out of 46.5 million disabilities held by 6,338,253 veterans receiving compensation (same report).

31.5%

Establishedof veterans had chronic pain in 2019, against 20.1% of nonveterans. At ages 20 to 34 it was 27.1% against 9.4% (CDC, National Health Interview Survey).

48%

Establishedof post-9/11 veterans said readjusting to civilian life was difficult, against 21% of earlier veterans (Pew Research Center, 2019).

Bodies damaged in service

EstablishedStrain of the lower back or neck accounts for another 1,791,869 service-connected disabilities, limited motion of the arm for 1,385,549, and limited motion of the ankle for 1,273,110 (VA, fiscal year 2025). In a national survey, 43.6% of veterans reported joint pain and 32.8% back pain, and severe pain was more common in veterans than in nonveterans, 9.1% against 6.3% (Nahin, Journal of Pain, 2017, as summarized by the NIH). Among active-duty service members, physician-diagnosed osteoarthritis occurred at 7.86 cases per 1,000 person-years from 1999 to 2008, which the authors reported as significantly higher than in comparable ages of the general population (Cameron and colleagues, Arthritis and Rheumatism, 2011, as summarized by the publisher).

HMR's viewYears of running long distances under heavy packs load a tensegrity unevenly, and the knees show it first. These are the bodies the protocol should be able to help, and the improvement should be measurable.

Discipline, mission, and what is owed

HMR's viewThe protocol is demanding. It asks for hours a day, every day, for a year. People trained to push through hard conditions for a shared purpose are the people most likely to complete it and to report on it accurately.

HMR's viewService gave these men and women something most people never have: a real mission, shared with others. Many lose it on the day they leave, and return to pain and to a society with no common purpose. Pew found that about a third of veterans at least sometimes struggled with the lack of structure in civilian life. This trial offers a new mission: repair your own body, learn the protocol well enough to teach it, then carry it to others.

HMR's viewThey were injured serving the rest of us. If this works, they should be the first to benefit, and the first to lead. The working name for the veteran community is Warriors Against Invisible Suffering.

EstablishedThe same facts create a duty of care. United States research rules require extra safeguards when participants may be vulnerable to coercion or undue influence, and an offer of a year of housing and food is a strong inducement for a veteran in need (45 CFR 46.111). The ethics section says how the design answers this.

3. Selection

Who is chosen, and how

We are transparent about the aim: a group that works well together, wants the mission, and leaves no excuses at the end. If the trial fails, it should not be because people did not get along or did not try.

Step one

An alignment survey

Applicants read the model and answer a survey on how they see it and its values. The trial tests a way of living, so participants need to want to live it for a year.

OpenSelecting for alignment has a cost to the science: people who expect a benefit tend to report one. The comparison group and the blinded assessors are there to limit this.

Step two

Collaboration and personality

A questionnaire on how each person handles conflict, cooperates, and prefers to live, and a personality assessment. These guide who shares a room. Applicants can name people they would like to room with.

Step three

Health and history

A detailed health questionnaire and medical screening before arrival, so the study physician can confirm that the water work and the daily load are safe for each person.

Balance

Roughly half women, half men

The target is close to 50/50 by sex, with the rest of the group as near to the veteran population as selection allows. Stating the target in advance, and its scientific reason, is what makes it reviewable.

Languages

Speakers for later deployment

Graduates are meant to carry the protocol abroad. The map of future sites sets the languages needed. For example, about 50 Hindi speakers for a first team in India.

Skills

About 50 skilled roles

Computing and AI engineers, building engineers and builders, kitchen leads, and about 20 certified dog trainers. They keep the campus running and teach their skills to anyone who signs up.

Cohort 1

Adults without children on site

The first cohort is for adults who can commit to living on campus for the year. Families join in cohort 2.

Dogs

Friendly dogs are welcome

A veteran with a friendly dog is welcome to bring it. Dogs with a history of fights are not accepted. Trainers assess every dog on arrival.

Values

Respect for everyone in the room

Rooms are assigned by sex unless a participant asks otherwise. Applicants agree to live respectfully beside people of every orientation and identity. No one is selected or rejected for their own orientation, identity, or religion.

Selected participants have their travel to the campus paid. Before arrival each receives a tablet account, orders uniforms within a set budget, and records food preferences.

4. The facility

A campus beside a Southern California base

The first campus sits next to a major military base, in a place where people can be outdoors all year. Residents and people on the base can visit each other, and the base may be able to host what the campus lacks, such as a hall for the first day.

Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton

EstablishedMore than 125,000 acres on the coast, about 38 miles north of downtown San Diego. More than 42,000 active-duty personnel, and more than 77,000 military retirees within 50 miles. Nearby towns include Oceanside, Fallbrook, Vista, and Carlsbad (Camp Pendleton, base information).

What the guidance says

Beds in a room

EstablishedWe looked for a study that gives an ideal number of beds for a shared room and did not find one. A review of United States Army living-space rules concluded that convincing evidence for a minimal or optimal space per soldier "has never been obtained" (Brodkey and Gaydos, Military Medicine, 1980). Navy and Marine Corps criteria house recruits in open bays with at most 140 square feet of building per person, and house permanent personnel two to a room (FC 4-721-10N).

EstimateOur rooms give 70 square feet of room per person and 200 of building per person. OpenTen to a room, in 100 rooms, would double the dormitory area.

A room for 1,000

EstablishedThe International Building Code, as adopted by states, counts 15 square feet a person at tables and chairs, 7 with chairs only, and 5 standing. A space holding 501 to 1,000 people needs three exits, and one holding more than 1,000 needs four (Table 1004.5 and Section 1006.2.1.1, Washington State's adoption).

EstimateOne hall seating all 1,000 at tables needs 15,000 square feet. With staff the count passes 1,000, so four exits. We plan two halls of 500 and an outdoor pavilion of 10,000 square feet for full gatherings. California's own code and the local fire marshal govern the final design.

The largest plants

EstablishedThe Ras Al-Khair plant in Saudi Arabia produces 1,036,000 cubic meters of water a day from eight multi-stage flash distillation units and 17 reverse osmosis units (Wikipedia). The Carlsbad plant, in one of the towns beside Camp Pendleton, makes more than 50 million gallons a day by reverse osmosis and cost about $1 billion as a project (San Diego County Water Authority).

EstimateThe campus needs about 50,000 to 75,000 gallons a day, near 190 to 285 cubic meters. That is several thousand times smaller than the largest plant. A record-size distillery is not needed.

6. A day in the life

One day, from 6:00 to lights down

The day is a community standard and not an order. Anyone can go back to their room at any time. The protocol's floor is three short showers and one immersion of 30 minutes or more each day.

EstimateThe wake time and the order of the day are from the design. The clock times after breakfast are our draft to make the order fit.

6:00

Wake, and ten minutes at the stretch station. Feet on the mat beside the bunk, eyes closed, hanging and stretching from the bars. Three questions, in order: what did I dream, how did yesterday go, and what do I want from today.

6:10

Restroom, teeth, water. Then a walk to the shower rooms with the tablet.

6:30

Shower one.Water 1 of 3 Clothes go in the laundry bin and the tablet in the dry box. Press the button: two minutes of warm water with two exfoliating sponges, working from the head down. A tone, then one minute of water carrying a dilute moisturizer. A second tone, then warm air dries the whole room while the sponges spread the water. Sponges go in their bin. Clean, folded clothes are waiting in the cubby.

6:45

Breakfast, brought to the table. The food was ordered last night. Sit anywhere, the tablet reports the table number, and the kitchen brings the tray. Drinks are water, cold-pressed juice, and smoothies from the hydration wall. Fresh fruit is always out. At the table, sign up for two morning activities and two afternoon activities, or one long field trip.

8:00

Morning activities. Two blocks of about 90 minutes. Some work on the body directly: assisted stretching, the obstacle course, yoga, the pool. Some use the body: gardening, building, dog training, a hike, art, a class in AI, or a visit to the base.

11:15

Shower two.Water 2 of 3 The same three-minute cycle, and a fresh set of clothes.

12:00

Lunch. The same table service. Announcements and music from the stage.

1:30

Afternoon activities, and the long immersion.30 minutes or more Each resident belongs to a numbered group, and each group has a daily slot in the tank rooms. Sessions follow a guided routine of 15 minutes, 30 minutes, or longer. Time in the pool counts as well.

4:45

Shower three.Water 3 of 3 People who have not worked up a sweat may skip one. The skip is recorded, and it becomes part of the data on dose.

5:30

Dinner.

7:00

The evening, by community vote. A rotating default, such as a film, a gathering in the garden, a long walk, yoga, a dance, a competition, trivia, or singing. Each day residents vote to keep the default or choose another. Taking part is optional.

8:30

The nightly order. On the tablet, choose tomorrow's meals so the kitchen cooks only what will be eaten. Every meal includes at least one piece of fruit.

EstimateThree showers at 2.5 gallons a minute, the standard showerhead flow (EPA WaterSense), come to 22.5 gallons a person each day, or 22,500 gallons for the campus. The moisturizer minute, at 2% of the flow, uses about 150 gallons of cream a day. That quantity has to be priced and tested before it is promised.

7. Community design

A community that runs on standards, signals, and shared work

The second experiment is the community itself: what daily life looks like when a thousand people share HMR's values and a mission.

Signals

Mood bands

Two bead bands, worn by choice. They tell others how to approach you before a word is said.

Left wristHow I feel today.
Right wristHow I want to be treated today.

One right-wrist color asks for company and celebration. Another asks to be left alone. The mess hall has a quiet section to match. Dogs wear a band as well: friendly, shy, or give me space.

Belonging

Uniforms

Everyone wears the same comfortable range of clothing with their name on it, as in the services, where it brings people together. A campus store offers choices within the range, including swimwear and one dressier set. Clean clothes are folded and waiting after every shower.

Tools

A tablet with Claude

Each resident has a tablet with a microphone button, an AI assistant, and a community dashboard: a profile, messages, the day's sign-ups, food orders, votes, and personal progress. Each person sets how open they are to messages.

Governance

Standards, not rigid rules

A thousand people with one mission set community standards together, such as the wake time and meal times, and can change them by vote. Residents elect an advocate who speaks for them.

Work

Jobs by sign-up

Residents run the campus: laundry, cooking, gardening, cleaning, and building. Each applicant says in advance what they are open to, and chooses at least one. The dashboard can call for help, for example when the night kitchen is short a person.

Waste

Scales under every bin

Solid food waste goes in one bin and liquid in another, each on a scale. The community sees its total each day and aims to throw away no more than about 5% of its food. Food is served on trays, and each person keeps two steel cups, one for water and one for everything else.

Joy

Weekly songs and dances

The community learns a shared set of songs, and holds a dance each week. One day a week is a relaxed day.

Making

Builders and artists

Builders add to the campus through the year, including a hand-carved wooden hall that anyone may carve on. Artists fill the walls, and string lights are placed with care. By the end of the year the place should look like the people who lived there.

Dogs

A community of dogs

Trainers score each dog. Calm dogs with reliable recall may be off leash. Others stay on leash or use the single lanes. Owners can sign up for training as a daily activity.

Ways to spend less

Lease, or use what the base has

Lease land or empty barracks from a base, or receive land through a government partnership, and use a base hall for orientation. EstimateRenovating leased buildings at 40% of the low building cost, with 40 tanks and the base's pool, brings the total to about $57 million.

Residents do the work

Laundry, cooking, cleaning, gardening, and building are done by the community, with a small paid team. The food garden lowers the food bill over the year.

One room, several uses

Push the mess hall tables to the wall and it becomes the dance floor and the cinema. The immersive room is a plain box with four projectors.

Shower, tank, and pool water is collected, treated, and reused, so the campus buys far less water.

Simpler tanks

Leave out the water jets and in-tank pumps. Keep sound and air.

Durable basics

Trays in place of plates, and two steel cups a person in place of disposables.

HMR's viewTwo things are not for cutting: the quality of the food and the safety staff. Healthy, fresh food is a condition of starting. People who are miserable will not finish a year, and the result would mean less.

EstimateA finding on water. A tank 5 feet across and 9 feet deep holds about 1,320 gallons. Draining and refilling a tank after every use would take about 1.3 million gallons a day, more than 25 times all other campus water, and distilling that with heat is not practical. The workable design filters and recirculates tank water, as a pool does, and replaces it on a schedule. Distilling 50,000 gallons a day in one pass needs about 119,000 kilowatt-hours of heat, using the textbook heat of vaporization of water. With solar collectors that would cover about 10 acres under our assumptions. A multi-stage design that reuses its heat about eight times would need a little over one acre. These figures need an engineer.

10. Ethics, consent, and safety

What the rules require, and the design that would pass

A clinician will read this section first. Research with people in the United States must be approved by an Institutional Review Board, which applies federal rules. Some parts of the original design would not pass as first drawn. We show the preference, the rule, and the design that would pass, side by side.

Consent is free and can be withdrawn

EstablishedConsent must be sought in ways that minimize coercion or undue influence. Participants must be told that taking part is voluntary and that they may stop at any time without penalty or loss of benefits (45 CFR 46.116).

Risks are minimized and privacy protected

EstablishedA board approves a study only if risks are minimized and reasonable, selection is equitable, privacy is adequately protected, and extra safeguards cover people vulnerable to undue influence (45 CFR 46.111).

Housing and food as an inducement

EstablishedFederal guidance warns that rewards can become undue influence, most of all for people in economic need, and that benefits should not be held back to the end (Office for Human Research Protections).

The design that would pass: anyone who leaves early gets paid travel home and help with housing and care. Nothing is forfeited by leaving.

Recording in bathrooms and showers the largest conflict
The preference

Video everywhere except toilet stalls and showers, with audio kept on in bathroom areas, so that no moment goes unrecorded.

What the rules require

EstablishedCalifornia makes it a crime to record a confidential communication without the consent of all parties (Penal Code 632). Staff, visitors, and the person on the other end of a phone call have not consented. A review board must also find privacy adequately protected.

Consent to this would have to be withdrawable at any moment, which a fixed microphone cannot honor.

The design that would pass

No audio and no video in toilets, showers, changing areas, the clinic, or private call rooms. Emergency call buttons and fall sensors cover safety there. Shower use is logged by the shower's own button and timer, which records the protocol without recording the person.

Cameras everywhere else
The preference

Full video and audio of all shared spaces, analyzed by AI, to capture what other trials miss between clinic visits.

What the rules require

EstablishedAdequate privacy and confidentiality provisions, and consent that states how far identifying records are kept confidential (45 CFR 46.111 and 46.116).

The design that would pass

Cameras in shared spaces only, with clear signs. Sleeping rooms are opt-in by every person in the room. Access to footage is limited and logged, with a set deletion date. Phone calls are never recorded. A participant can ask for their footage to be removed from analysis.

Public livestreams of participants
The preference

Live public streams from many cameras, so the public can follow the trial as it happens.

What the rules require

EstablishedThe privacy and confidentiality duties above. A live stream of identifiable research participants, some with medical conditions, cannot be taken back once seen.

OpenWe know of no residential clinical trial that has streamed its participants. A board is unlikely to approve it as first drawn.

The design that would pass

A public dashboard of group progress with no faces or names. Scheduled broadcasts of events, with a separate media release from each person shown, a delay, and the right to say no without leaving the study. Openness comes from publishing the protocol, the data plan, and the results in full.

Immersion tanks with breathing tubes
The preference

Tanks 6.5 to 9 feet deep, fully submerged, breathing through a tube, with no attendant required and an emergency drain button.

What the rules require

EstablishedWater pressure rises by about one atmosphere for every 10 meters of depth, so the deeper the chest, the harder the lungs must work to pull surface air down a tube, and at 10 meters it cannot be done (University of Illinois Physics). Air supplied under pressure solves that and adds a new risk: a held breath during a rise of even 5 feet can injure the lungs and cause an arterial gas embolism (Divers Alert Network). People with seizure disorders or heart conditions are at higher risk of drowning (CDC).

The design that would pass

A trained attendant in the room for every session, never AI alone, and no solo use. Each tank has an emergency drain, an exit ladder, and a release the attendant can reach. Medical screening before first use. Start with head-out or shallow immersion. Any breathing equipment is designed and supervised with diving medicine specialists, and residents are trained before using it.

Pool supervision
The preference

No lifeguard. AI watches the pool and sounds a campus alarm, and the community responds.

What the rules require

EstablishedCalifornia requires lifeguard service at public pools that charge a direct fee, and signs at other public pools that have none (Health and Safety Code 116045). Public pools must be disinfected continuously, with at least 1.0 part per million of free chlorine or 2.0 of bromine (22 CCR 65529). OpenWhether the campus pool counts as a public pool is for the county to decide.

The design that would pass

Certified lifeguards on duty whenever the pool is open. Residents can earn the certificate and take shifts. AI detection is a second layer. The pool is closed and locked when no guard is present. Water treatment follows the state rule, which affects the plan for an unchlorinated pool.

Selection by belief and by sexual orientation
The preference

A group aligned with HMR and comfortable with people of every orientation and identity, with no discomfort left to blame if the trial fails.

What the rules require

EstablishedSelection of participants must be equitable (45 CFR 46.111). California's civil rights law guarantees equal treatment in all business establishments regardless of religion, sex, sexual orientation, and other characteristics (Civil Code 51). OpenHow that law applies to a research campus is a question for counsel.

The design that would pass

No one is chosen or refused for their religion, orientation, or identity. Every applicant consents to the community's values: respect for all residents and shared rooms assigned without regard to orientation. Selection criteria are written down, tied to the science or the mission, and reviewed by the board.

Giving up phones
The preference

Personal phones and devices are handed in. The tablet holds the contact details of loved ones.

What the rules require

EstablishedParticipation is voluntary and may end at any time without penalty (45 CFR 46.116). A participant who cannot reach the outside world in private cannot freely exercise that right.

The design that would pass

Phones are stored by choice and returned on request, at once. Private, unrecorded calls to family are always available. Every participant can reach the review board, an independent advocate, and crisis services directly, without going through study staff.

Alcohol
The preference

Alcohol sold one day a week at the pool.

What the rules require

EstablishedAlcohol is involved in up to 70% of deaths associated with water recreation (CDC).

The design that would pass

If alcohol is offered at all, it is away from the water, in limited amounts, with no pool or tank use afterward that day. Some residents will be in recovery, so alcohol-free events are the default. The principal investigator decides.

Distilled water only for drinking
The preference

All water on campus starts as distilled, including drinking water, with minerals coming from food and juice, or from an added electrolyte mix.

What the rules require

EstablishedA review prepared for the World Health Organization reports that water very low in minerals increases the loss of sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium, and that drinking several liters of it after hard exertion can cause acute low blood sodium. It cites recommended minimums of 100 milligrams a liter of dissolved solids, 10 of magnesium, and 20 to 30 of calcium (Kozisek, WHO, 2004). Turning used water into drinking water is tightly regulated in California (State Water Board, 2024).

The design that would pass

Drinking water is distilled and then remineralized to current medical guidance, as the design's electrolyte option allows. Electrolytes are standard after exertion. Recycled water serves showers, tanks, and gardens. Drinking water comes from the public supply unless the state approves otherwise.

No-deodorant guidance
The preference

HMR's viewBody odor is a sign that the fascia needs a rinse. The store would not stock deodorant, which also clouds the recycled water.

What the rules require

EstablishedBody odor comes mainly from skin microbes breaking down compounds in sweat, which is itself nearly odorless (American Society for Microbiology, 2021). No law is at issue. A board would object to any rule that shames a participant.

The design that would pass

Guidance, never a ban. The store carries fragrance-free products, and no one is singled out. Whether frequent rinsing changes odor can be measured as a small study of its own.

Dogs and allergies
The preference

Many dogs, welcome almost everywhere. People allergic to dogs are advised not to apply.

What the rules require

EstablishedAbout 12% of people in a United States survey tested positive for dog or cat allergy antibodies, and no breed is truly free of allergens (Chan and Leung, 2018). Excluding all of them narrows who can take part, which a board weighs under equitable selection.

The design that would pass

Dog-free dormitory rooms, a dog-free mess section, and dog-free shower and tank rooms. Vaccination and health checks for every dog, bite reporting, and insurance. Allergy is disclosed at screening so each person can decide with the study physician.

Also required before a first participant

A principal investigator and a home institution

A qualified clinical researcher who takes responsibility for the study, and an institution whose review board oversees it.

Medical cover on site

A study physician, nurses around the clock, screening before arrival, and a plan for injuries and mental health crises, with treatment for study-related injury spelled out in the consent form.

An independent safety board

OpenFor a year-long study of this size we expect reviewers to ask for an independent group that sees the data during the year and can pause the study.
