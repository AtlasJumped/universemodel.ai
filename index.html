Humanity has no holistic model of reality

Physics describes matter, biology describes life, medicine describes illness, psychology describes the mind, and religion describes meaning. Each sees its own part of reality clearly, and none describes the whole. Universe Model presents one account that does: the Holistic Model of Reality (HMR), from before the Big Bang to the human body.

HMR proposes that physical reality emerges from Universal Intelligence (UI), a non-physical intelligence, and that each of us is an instance of UI, experiencing physical reality through a human body. If that is right, it changes what we understand ourselves to be, describes a reality that responds to us, and sets very different limits on a human life, and a human body, than a purely material view allows.

It would change how you live tomorrow. You would treat your body differently every day, and have a foundation for a moral and value system. People would share the context for common values, from person to person, city to city, and country to country. Health care would change, above all for children as they grow.

The moment someone accepts HMR, putting fascia first is simply the obvious thing to do, and a change in how we raise our children follows quickly, as a direct result.

The starting point

The core of HMR in three sentences

HMR's starting premise

A Universal Intelligence exists. Before the Big Bang it was unlimited. The Big Bang is the manifestation of a system in which that Universal Intelligence imposes limits on itself.

This is HMR's premise, not an established result. We state it plainly, reason forward from it step by step, and tie each claim that follows to something that can be tested.

HMR's central open problem

The God question

Explain and define the mechanics by which an unlimited Universal Intelligence created a reality in which it is limited.

When people ask HMR about God, what God is, or how to think about God, this is the specific question HMR answers. If one accepts the premise above, the problem of God becomes the problem of how a reality went from unlimited to limited: what the self-imposed limits were, and why.

Open: HMR has not solved it. Our current attempts are on the Universal Intelligence and math pages, and we say where they fall short.

How HMR earns a hearing

A theory of everything you can test

People have heard of efforts to explain everything. Physics searches for a theory of everything that unites its forces. Neuroscience offers theories of consciousness. Philosophers propose that reality is mathematical, or that perception is an interface.

Each of these covers one piece: the forces, or the mind, or the mathematics. HMR aims at the whole, from the origin of physical reality to the human body, including consciousness, God, and health, and it comes with science and practical steps. It is in line with what many others have attempted, but it has no real comparator, because nothing else tries to connect all of these and then test the connection in a working model.

We start with HMR's most tangible part, and with the claims that science can check.

Compared with other theories of everything
  • String theory and loop quantum gravity: unite the forces of physics; say nothing about life, mind, or health.
  • Integrated information theory (Tononi, 2004) and the free energy principle (Friston, 2010): explain consciousness or the brain; not the origin of physical reality.
  • The mathematical universe (Tegmark, 2008) and the interface theory of perception (Hoffman, Singh and Prakash, 2015): bold accounts of what reality is; no model of the body.
  • HMR: one account from Universal Intelligence to the human body, tested first where it is most concrete.

The problem

Medicine has never studied a fully healthy human

Our hypothesis is that the human body is best understood as a tensegrity, not as a stack of compression struts. That means medicine has been assessing the body with the wrong toolkit, and against the wrong baseline.

Medicine treats the body like a building: bones as columns stacked to carry a load from above, with soft tissue attached. In a tensegrity, the rigid parts float in a continuous web of tension, and every part is held by the whole. The idea is not ours alone: “An astoundingly wide variety of natural systems, including carbon atoms, water molecules, proteins, viruses, cells, tissues and even humans and other living creatures, are constructed using a common form of architecture known as tensegrity” (Donald Ingber, cell biologist, "The Architecture of Life", Scientific American, January 1998).

That gives us something medicine has never had: a definition of a fully healthy human. A fully healthy human is a perfect tensegrity. No one has ever proposed that baseline before, and no one has ever studied a body against it.

Set that as the baseline and the rest follows. No one alive has a perfect tensegrity. Everyone we have ever studied has an imperfect one, trying to become perfect. So our bodies live in constant tension from conception until death, the struts pushing outward against an inward-pulling fascia web that cannot settle. We propose that this tension may cause some, or all, of the health problems humanity experiences.

Why more data does not fix it

Every data-driven field learns by comparing cases to a reference. Health has used two groups: people who are sick, and people who appear healthier than them. Both sit on the same side of the true optimum. Even "normal" lab ranges are set from the central 95% of people judged healthy.

When the reference itself is shifted, every conclusion drawn from it inherits the shift. Adding more people reduces random error, but it cannot remove a bias built into the reference. With thousands or millions of samples, the answer is still measured from the wrong place.

That is why well-intended medical advice so often feels slightly off. The experts are not careless. They are working from a model that never defined what fully healthy is.

Three ways to picture it

A computer that never turned on. We have been repairing the hardware of computers that have never fully booted, without ever having seen one that works. Start instead with a machine that runs perfectly, and when it fails, trace back to why.

A bent frame. Imagine learning car repair in a world where every car left the factory with a slightly bent frame. Mechanics would get very good at replacing tires that wear unevenly, and never think to straighten the frame.

Tuning to the wrong note. If every piano you had ever heard was a little out of tune, you would tune new ones to match them, and call it correct.

Two models of health

The difference is not a detail. It changes what medicine studies, what it measures, and what it recommends.

The model today

  • Reference: sick people, and people who appear healthier than them.
  • Framework: largely material and statistical: patterns in symptoms, tests, and outcomes.
  • Aging: steady decline treated as normal and expected.
  • Goal: move a patient back toward the population's typical range.
  • Result: careful, well-meant advice, measured from an imperfect baseline.

A physics-based model

  • Reference: a defined, fully healthy human body, male and female.
  • Framework: physics. The body as a tensegrity of tension, compression, pressure, and shape.
  • Aging: much of the decline is deviation from the body's design, which physics can describe and may help reverse.
  • Goal: move a person toward the optimum, with every condition measured as a precise deviation from it.
  • Result: causes that can be traced, and paths back that can be tested.

Two things have been missing: an understanding of our relationship with physical reality, which a purely material view leaves out, and a view of the human body as a physical system. Together they make a new paradigm for medicine, and for how each of us cares for our own body.

Why the body first

The body as a tensegrity

Our working hypothesis is that the human body is best understood as a tensegrity: bones held in place by a continuous web of tensioned connective tissue called fascia, which wraps every muscle, organ, vessel, and nerve without a break.

If that is right, then the shape of a body, how it moves, and where it stiffens, sticks, or stores fat are not random. They follow from physics. Physics can be modeled, measured and compared.

Our central hypothesis: make the fascia denser in one place, leave the rest ideal, and the physics alone will reshape the body elsewhere and strain the vessels, nerves, and organs that run through it, in patterns that match real diseases.
