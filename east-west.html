East and West

One reality, described in many languages.

Long before laboratories, people sat in caves and forests with nothing but their bodies and their minds, and studied both for a lifetime. The HMR holds that what they found, about the body and about reality, fits with modern science, and that what has been missing is a shared language.

A key goal of the HMR is to provide that language.

The body

Practices that work on the fascia web.

Without anatomy books, contemplatives mapped the body from the inside: the bandhas, or "locks", of hatha yoga; the channels of prana called nadis; the meridians and qi of Chinese medicine; the slow, spiraling movements of tai chi and qigong; the marma points of Ayurveda.

The HMR's view is that these traditions were working, with great precision, on the fascia web, the body's continuous connective tissue: holding, lengthening, hydrating and releasing fascia through breath, posture, pressure and attention. Science is beginning to look in the same places. One anatomical study found that acupuncture points and meridians correspond closely to the planes of connective tissue between and within muscles (Langevin and Yandow, 2002).

Reality

Inquiry that turned inward.

The same traditions reached conclusions about the nature of reality that the HMR shares: that what we call the world rests on something deeper, conscious and whole, and that each person is an expression of it. They also studied the sky and developed mathematics of remarkable depth, from Indian astronomy and the use of zero to the calendars of the Maya.

The HMR suggests the two discoveries are connected. As practitioners came into closer contact with their own bodies, they also came closer to the nature of reality itself.

Where the maps meet

Chakras, meridians and the lines of fascia.

The traditions' maps of the body and modern maps of fascia are starting to be compared. Some correspondences are already measured; others are noted but not yet tested.

Measured

Meridians follow fascial planes

Mapping the arm, researchers found that about 80% of acupuncture points, and most meridian paths, lie along planes of connective tissue between or within muscles (Langevin and Yandow, 2002). A later review proposed the fascia network as the anatomical basis of acupoints and meridians (Bai et al., 2011).

Measured

Lines of fascia carry force

Modern anatomy describes long myofascial chains running the length of the body. A systematic review of dissection studies found strong evidence for three of them, including the superficial back line from the soles of the feet to the brow (Wilke et al., 2016), echoing the long channels mapped by yoga and Chinese medicine.

Noted, to be tested

Chakras and the body's junctions

The traditional chakras sit along the body's midline at places of anatomical convergence: near the major nerve plexuses, the diaphragm, the throat and the pelvic floor, where layers of fascia meet and the bandhas are applied. The correspondence is often noted but has not yet been rigorously studied. It is one the HMR aims to test.

The hidden variable

Timing: why slow movement reaches fascia differently.

Most of what people do with their bodies happens at one speed: the speed of ordinary life. The traditions changed the speed. They held positions for minutes, moved slower than anyone moves by accident, or repeated a motion in a steady rhythm. The HMR's view is that timing is one of the main ways these practices reach the fascia.

Measured

Fascia answers to time

Fascia is viscoelastic: under a sustained load it keeps lengthening slowly, and under a sustained stretch its tension slowly eases. A quick stretch and a long hold are different events to the tissue. In connective tissue held stretched, the cells themselves expand and remodel within minutes (Langevin et al., 2013), and fascia's own contractile cells act over minutes to months, not seconds (Schleip and Klingler, 2019).

Measured

Slow pressure reaches different sensors

Fascia is rich in nerve endings. Some respond best to slow, sustained pressure and lateral stretch rather than quick movement, and are linked to calming of the nervous system (Schleip, 2003). Moving slowly does not just do less of the same thing; it speaks to different receptors.

Hypothesis

A calibration session for the mind

When movement is far slower than anything in daily life, as in tai chi, the mind cannot run its usual automatic patterns. It has to attend to each moment of tension. The HMR proposes that this puts the mind into a calibration session, in which it can sense and reach the fascia in a way it normally cannot, and that every slow Eastern practice works partly this way.

Ordinary life

  • Speed: quick, habitual, mostly unattended.
  • Holds: seconds, rarely more.
  • Effect on fascia: the same lines loaded the same way, every day, reinforcing whatever pattern is already there.

The traditions

  • Speed: slowed to a crawl (tai chi, qigong), or set by the breath (yoga flows).
  • Holds: minutes (yin yoga, standing meditation, long asana holds).
  • Effect on fascia, as the HMR proposes: time for the tissue to creep and release, new sensors engaged, and attention brought to tension the mind normally ignores.
Practices and what they do

They were onto something, without the words we have now.

The traditions described their methods in the language of breath, energy and spirit. Read through the anatomy of fascia, many of them look like precise ways of loading, releasing and sensing the connective-tissue web. These readings are the HMR's hypotheses, to be tested in the simulation.

Hatha yoga

The bandhas: three internal locks

  • Mula bandha, the root lock: a sustained lift of the pelvic floor, the sheet of muscle and fascia that closes the bottom of the trunk.
  • Uddiyana bandha, the abdominal lock: after a full exhale, the belly is drawn in and up under the ribs, lifting the respiratory diaphragm and stretching the fascia of the abdominal wall and the back of the trunk.
  • Jalandhara bandha, the throat lock: the chin is drawn down toward the breastbone, lengthening the back of the neck and compressing the front, where the layers of the neck's fascia meet the top of the chest.
  • Maha bandha: all three held together.

Osteopaths describe three horizontal "diaphragms" where the body's vertical fascia is crossed by sheets of tissue: the pelvic floor, the breathing diaphragm and the top of the chest. The three bandhas act on exactly those three places. The HMR reads them as a method for tensioning and releasing the trunk's fascia from the inside out.

Tai chi and qigong

Slow spirals and standing still

Tai chi moves the whole body continuously in slow spirals, with the weight shifting from leg to leg. Spiral movement loads the long diagonal lines of fascia that wrap the trunk and limbs, and the slowness gives the tissue time to respond (see Timing).

Zhan zhuang, "standing like a post", holds one posture for many minutes. Nothing appears to happen, but the body must keep making tiny adjustments to stay upright, and the fascia carries the posture for minutes at a time instead of seconds.

Yoga, breath and holds

Long holds, breath and inversion

Long-held postures, as in yin yoga, place a low, steady load on connective tissue for minutes, the time scale on which fascia creeps and its cells remodel. Pranayama, breath control, moves the diaphragm through its full range and changes pressure inside the trunk with every breath. Inversions reverse the pull of gravity on the whole web.

Sun salutations link the front and back lines of the body in one repeated sequence, timed to the breath.

Outside in

Pressure, scraping and suction

Many traditions also work on fascia from the outside. Gua sha scrapes the skin along lines of the body; cupping lifts the skin and tissue beneath it with suction; Thai and Chinese massage apply slow pressure along the meridians; Ayurvedic marma therapy presses specific points.

In the HMR, these are outside-in tools for the same web the bandhas and slow movement reach from the inside. Each is most useful as part of a whole protocol, not as a single technique tested alone.

This describes research hypotheses, not instructions. Some of these practices are demanding; learn them from a qualified teacher.

The language of the HMR.

The HMR needs only a small set of terms. Humanity already has words for these things, but many of them are vague, overlapping, or tied to one tradition. The aim is to identify what is actually true and name it precisely, so that science and the traditions can be read in the same words.

Physical reality

Everything that can be measured: matter, energy, space and time, and the laws that govern them. What is usually just called "reality".

Universal Intelligence (UI)

The non-physical intelligence from which physical reality emerges, and the process by which it does so; the two are one. UI is another name for God, or for any higher power. Defining UI →

God

One of humanity's names for UI, alongside Brahman, the Tao, Allah and many others. The names differ; the source is the same.

Physical laws

The stable rules UI holds fixed so that many separate beings can share one physical reality. Science is the study of these rules.

Life

Any instance of UI experiencing physical reality, from a single cell or a blade of grass to a human being.

Human

An instance of UI embedded within physical reality, able to experience it through an interface: the human body. In Hindu thought, closest to Atman.

Body

The physical interface through which an instance of UI senses and acts in physical reality. In humans, a tensegrity held together by fascia.

Mind

The part of a human through which UI thinks, remembers, chooses and makes meaning within a single life.

Emotions

Felt signals, carried through body and mind, that report how an experience stands: calm or threat, joy or grief, connection or loss.

Consciousness

Experience itself. In the HMR it is fundamental: not produced by matter, but the ground from which matter emerges.

Coherence

Order, harmony and integration. The direction in which UI, and every life, tends to develop.

Death

A transition from experience within physical reality back to non-physical existence, in which the experience of that life is kept and added to UI's understanding of what physical life is like.

The HMR is not affiliated with any religion. Hindu terms appear because they are among the most precise available, not to place one tradition above the others. This vocabulary is a first version and will grow.

Many names, one source.

Viewed through the HMR, the world's traditions do not contradict each other. Each names and describes different aspects of the same Universal Intelligence (UI). An intelligence able to generate physical reality could be called by any name, and could be understood as one, as many, or as everything.

Hindu traditions

Brahman and Atman

The Upanishads teach that the self within is one with the ground of all being: "tat tvam asi", you are that. Yoga, its bandhas and pranayama, and the Kundalini tradition work through the body toward that recognition.

Buddhist traditions

Mind and awareness

Centuries of systematic introspection into attention, perception and suffering, with practices of breath and posture that train the body and mind together.

Taoist and Chinese traditions

The Tao and qi

An unnamed source from which all things arise, and a living energy moving along mapped channels, cultivated through tai chi, qigong and acupuncture.

Japanese and Tibetan traditions

Ki and the subtle body

Aikido and shiatsu work with ki; Tibetan yogas describe channels, winds and drops, practiced through breath and movement.

Abrahamic mystical traditions

The one behind the many

Kabbalah's Ein Sof, the infinite beyond description; Sufi practices of breath, movement and remembrance; the breath prayer of Christian contemplatives.

Greek and Western philosophy

The One and the whole

From Pythagoras and Plato to Plotinus and Spinoza, a line of thinkers who saw reality as one ordered whole, with mind at its root.

Indigenous traditions

A living world

Many Indigenous traditions describe a sacred, living intelligence present in all things, and practices that keep a person in right relation with it.

Modern science

The rules of physical reality

In the HMR, the laws of physics are the stable rules that make a complex physical reality possible at all. Science describes those rules; it is one more way of reading the same source.

The goal: a shared language.

The traditions had the practices and the insight, but not the anatomy. Science has the anatomy and the measurements, but no place for consciousness. The HMR aims to build the vocabulary that lets each be read in the terms of the other.

Fascia is where that work starts. If a physics-based model of the body can show why these practices work, it will also be the first step toward showing that the traditions were right about the nature of reality.

The body practices were the method. The fascia was the medium. The insight was about reality.
