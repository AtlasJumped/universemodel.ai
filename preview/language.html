Language

Writing clearly

A model of reality is only as clear as the words that carry it. This page lists the habits we keep, the phrases we cut, where commas go, what can be shortened, and the working terms we use because the usual words say too little.

Our habits

The rules this site writes by

Form
  • Short sentences. One idea in each.
  • Serial comma: "bones, fascia, and skin."
  • No en dash. Use a comma, a colon, or a new sentence.
  • No sentence starts with "And".
  • Titles carry no closing period.
Voice
  • American English: color, center, behavior, organized.
  • "We", never "I".
  • Plain words over long ones.
  • No remark about how careful or honest the page is being. Be careful and honest, and leave the remark out.
Claims
  • Mark each claim once, where it is made, as established science, HMR's view, or open.
  • Established science carries a source link.
  • HMR's view reads as HMR's: "In HMR..." or "HMR holds...".
  • Open means we do not know yet. Say so in those words.
Cut these

Words that add length and no meaning

Four kinds turn up most. Each pair below is an invented example.

Filler

It is really quite important to note that fascia is basically everywhere in the body.

Fascia is everywhere in the body.

Words like really, quite, basically, very, and just rarely carry meaning. Cut them first.

Stacked hedges

It would seem that this may perhaps suggest a possible link.

This may be linked. (Or: this is linked, and here is the source.)

One hedge is enough. If the claim has a label, the label does the work.

Caveats that sound automatic

This is the model's premise, not an established result. We state it plainly, and readers should weigh it accordingly.

In HMR, Universal Intelligence comes first.

The words "In HMR" already say whose claim it is. A second sentence that repeats the point, or that announces how frankly we write, adds nothing.

"In which" and "by which" chains

The process by which the body distributes load is one in which every part is linked to the others.

The body distributes load through linked parts.

When a sentence needs "in which" twice, split it, or find the verb hiding inside the noun.

Announcing

In this section we will now go on to discuss the three main kinds of exercise.

Exercise comes in three main kinds.

Do the thing. Do not say that you are about to.

Empty openers

It is worth pointing out that there are many people who stretch every day.

Many people stretch every day.

"There is", "there are", and "it is" often push the real subject to the end.

Commas

Where a comma goes, and where it does not

Use a comma
  • In a list of three or more, before "and" or "or." Bones, fascia, and skin. The last comma stops "fascia and skin" from reading as one item.
  • Between two full sentences joined by "and," "but," or "so." The model is simple, and it can be tested.
  • After a long opening phrase. When the body is loaded unevenly, the weak line fails first.
  • Around extra information that can be dropped. Fascia, which wraps every muscle, holds its shape under load. Use "which" here.
  • In numbers of four digits or more. 1,000 veterans.
Leave it out
  • Between a subject and its verb. Not: The web of fascia around the bones, holds them apart.
  • When the second half has no subject of its own. The model is simple and can be tested.
  • After a short opening phrase. Today we test it. Last year it failed.
  • Before a clause the sentence needs. The fascia that wraps the muscle is thickest at the knee. Use "that" here, with no commas.
  • Between two sentences with nothing joining them. Not: It works, we tested it. Use a period or a colon.

Test for the "which" and "that" pair: if the clause can be removed and the sentence still says the same thing, it takes "which" and commas. If removing it changes what is meant, it takes "that" and none.

Words we need

HMR's working terms

These are HMR's choices of word, not rules of English. We use them because each carries more than the common word it replaces.

Original and deviation

Instead of "change." "Change" says only that something is different. "Deviation" says more: there was a standard, the original, and something departed from it. That makes the departure something we can measure and, where possible, close.

Example: "The knee shows a deviation from its original alignment" tells the reader what the reference is. "The knee has changed" does not.

System, situation, conditions
  • System for parts that act together, in place of the vague "thing" or "stuff."
  • Situation for how things stand at one moment.
  • Conditions for the circumstances that have to hold for something to happen.

Choose the one that fits, and keep it fixed. If a page says "system" for one thing, it should not call the same thing "setup" two paragraphs later.

Still open

English has gaps, and the list above is short. If you keep reaching for a word that does not exist, or use a long phrase for something that should have one word, write to us. A good new word names something we already need to say.

In short

We write short sentences, use the serial comma, avoid the en dash, and label each claim as established science, HMR's view, or open. We cut filler, stacked hedges, automatic caveats, and chains of "in which". We prefer original and deviation to change, because deviation says there was a standard.
