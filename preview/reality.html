Problems: science today

Where science falls short.

If you have come from the start of this site, you have met the idea that humanity's models of reality are partial. Science is the best of them, so this is the place to look first.

Modern science is the most successful description of physical reality humanity has ever had, and it deserves real thanks. Its branches describe the rules of physical reality with great precision. But the system as a whole has conflicts within it, and it cannot yet say what most of the universe is made of, what gravity is, what life is, or why anyone is conscious at all.

One of our two best theories, or the way they are combined, is wrong about something basic.

That is not an outsider's complaint. It follows from the mathematics on this page, and physicists say it themselves. Everything below is organized around it.

A math problem, not a gap in data

Where the mathematics of physics conflicts.

When two sets of equations, each confirmed by experiment, give contradictory or infinite answers about the same situation, one of two things is true. Either the math is wrong, or the framework that tells us what the math describes is wrong. Math problems cannot be explained away. These are the places where it happens.

1

Singularities

density → ∞, curvature → ∞

General relativity itself proves that, under very general conditions, collapsing stars and the early universe must reach points where its quantities become infinite (Penrose, 1965; Hawking and Penrose, 1970). Nothing physical can be infinitely dense: an infinity in an equation means the equation has stopped describing the world. So the theory predicts its own failure, at the center of every black hole and at the beginning of time.

2

Gravity cannot be made quantum

infinitely many unknown constants

The method that turned electromagnetism and the nuclear forces into quantum theories fails for gravity. Calculations produce infinities that cannot be absorbed into a finite set of measured numbers, so at high energies the theory predicts nothing (Goroff and Sagnotti, 1986). Physicists call gravity "non-renormalizable". In plain words: for the most extreme events in the universe, our two best theories together give no answer at all.

3

The energy of empty space

predicted ÷ measured ≈ 10120

Quantum theory says empty space is never truly empty: it always holds some energy. General relativity says that energy must push on the expansion of the universe. So physicists calculated how much there should be, and then measured it. The measured amount is tiny but not zero; the natural calculation gives a number about 10120 times larger, a 1 followed by 120 zeros (Weinberg, 1989). Explained below.

4

Two kinds of time

HΨ = 0 (no time variable)

Most of us live as if the world runs on one clock. Physics uses two different ideas of time. In quantum mechanics, time is a single fixed clock ticking in the background. In general relativity there is no such clock: time bends, and runs at different rates in different places. When the two theories are combined directly, time drops out of the central equation altogether (DeWitt, 1967). This is known as the problem of time.

5

Black holes and lost information

pure state → mixed state

A rule at the heart of quantum mechanics says information is never destroyed: in principle, you could always work backward from what is to what was. Hawking showed that black holes slowly evaporate, and his calculation implies that everything that fell in is erased for good (Hawking, 1976). Both results come from trusted theory, and they cannot both hold. Fifty years later the "information paradox" is still argued over.

The alternative framework

The math is not the problem. The framework is. Each equation here works superbly in its own domain. What fails is the frame they are read in: physics treats space, time and matter as the bottom layer of reality. The Holistic Model of Reality (HMR) is an alternative framework in which they are not the bottom layer; they emerge from Universal Intelligence (UI). The conflicts appear exactly where physics pushes back toward an origin (the Big Bang, the center of a black hole, empty space itself), which is what one would expect if the foundations sit one level deeper. What we use now

Newton, Einstein and the quantum.

Newton (1687) described gravity as a force: every mass pulls on every other mass, instantly, across empty space, inside a fixed space and a single universal clock. It is so accurate that it still flies spacecraft.

Einstein (1915) replaced that picture. In general relativity there is no force pulling across space. Mass and energy bend space and time, and objects follow the bends. Nothing, not even gravity, travels faster than light, and time runs at different rates in different places.

How they conflict. They disagree about what gravity is: an instant pull through a fixed stage, or a bending of the stage itself that spreads at a finite speed. In everyday conditions their numbers agree closely, which is why Newton is still used, but where gravity is strong or speeds are high only Einstein matches what we measure. Newton's picture did not survive; his arithmetic survives as an approximation.

The quantum (1900 to 1930) describes atoms, light and particles with extraordinary precision, and it pictures reality in yet another way: in discrete packets, as probabilities, on a fixed stage of space and time, the very stage Einstein showed is not fixed.

That last disagreement is the one that has never been resolved. Branches of physics describe the rules with great precision, but the system as a whole has conflicts within it. Where general relativity and quantum mechanics must both apply, they cannot both be right as they stand.

This matters beyond physics. Science is often held up as the settled standard that every other account of reality must answer to. Its methods have earned great respect. But a body of knowledge whose two foundations contradict each other is not a finished account of reality, and it is not a position from which to rule other accounts out. On the deepest questions, honesty requires the same humility from everyone.

In short

Each theory is confirmed in its own domain. Together they rest on different pictures of what reality is, and no one has combined them into one account.

The worst prediction in physics

A miss of 10120, in plain terms.

Take a box and remove everything from it: every atom, every particle of light. Quantum mechanics says something is still there. Empty space is never perfectly still; every field in it keeps a minimum jitter that cannot be removed, and that jitter carries energy.

Energy has weight, and Einstein's theory says weight bends space. So physicists can do two things: calculate how much energy empty space should hold, and measure, from how the universe moves, how much it actually holds. The measurement is tiny: in a cubic meter of empty space, about as much energy as is locked inside a few hydrogen atoms. The calculation is larger by a factor of roughly 10120, a 1 followed by 120 zeros (Weinberg, 1989).

To feel that number: it is as if a calculation of the weight of one grain of sand came out heavier than the entire observable universe, and not by a little, but by a factor with about 60 more zeros. With a vacuum that heavy, the universe would have flown apart or collapsed before a single star could form.

This is where the two best theories meet directly, and the answer is not slightly off. It is the clearest sign that one of them, or the way they are combined, is wrong about something basic.

The dark universe

We can identify about 5% of what the universe contains.

By the best current measurements, from the European Space Agency's Planck satellite, ordinary matter (every star, planet, gas cloud and living thing, everything made of atoms) is only about 5% of the universe's contents. About 27% is called dark matter and about 68% dark energy (Planck Collaboration, 2020; NASA).

"Dark" here does not describe a substance. It is a placeholder: a term for an entire chunk of reality we have no understanding of, and together the two placeholders take up about 95% of the universe's contents. Dark matter is the name for extra gravity that we observe but cannot trace to anything we can detect; decades of searches have not found a dark-matter particle. Dark energy is the name for whatever is making the expansion of the universe speed up. In both cases we can measure the effect and have no confirmed account of the cause. So when physicists say their nature is unknown, they mean it literally: about 95% of the contents of the universe are not understood.

Put it in school terms. A score of 90% is roughly an A. Below 50% is an F. Our understanding of what the universe is made of scores about 5%. That is not a nearly finished exam with a few hard questions left. It is a first page.

One more open question, noted lightly. "The universe is expanding" is itself an interpretation of the light from distant galaxies, which is stretched toward red. The usual reading is that space is growing. A minority of physicists have shown the same observations can be described as the things inside the universe, its particles and atoms, changing size instead (Wetterich, 2013; Nature news). The HMR finds that reading worth keeping open. It is a question, not a claim.

What "unknown" means here

Dark matter: we see its gravity pulling on stars and galaxies. We have never seen the thing doing the pulling.

Dark energy: we see the expansion of the universe speeding up. We do not know what is pushing it.

How the 5% is measured

The Planck satellite mapped the oldest light in the universe, released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The pattern of tiny hot and cold spots in that light depends on how much ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy there is, which is how the proportions are fixed so precisely, even though two of the three are unexplained.

The biggest gap

No one knows what gravity is.

Imagine a language that can describe everything in the world precisely, except one thing, and that one thing is under your feet every moment of your life. Quantum mechanics is that language. It is the most successful theory ever tested, and it describes every other known force. It cannot express gravity. Gravity is not a corner case: it shapes planets, stars, galaxies and the flow of time. A description of reality that cannot express its most basic force is missing something basic, and the missing piece could look very different from materialism.

  • It will not join the rest of physics. The other three forces have quantum descriptions. Gravity does not, and every attempt to give it one, from string theory to loop quantum gravity, remains unconfirmed.
  • Its mathematics breaks down where it matters most. At the center of a black hole and at the Big Bang, the equations of general relativity return infinite values for density and curvature. An infinity is not a measurement; it is the theory telling us it no longer describes what is happening. Where the math conflicts
  • It is absurdly weak. Between two protons, the electric force is about 1036 times stronger than gravity. No one knows why the difference is so enormous.
  • It does not add up. Galaxies spin and cluster as if they held far more mass than we can see. Either most of the matter in the universe is invisible, or our account of gravity is wrong at large scales.

What a material view has not explained

Open questions at the foundations.

None of these proves that materialism is wrong. Each is a place where a purely material account of reality has no agreed answer, and where some of the founders of modern physics themselves expected one to be needed.

Entanglement

Connected across any distance

Measure one of two entangled particles and the other's result is correlated with it instantly, however far apart they are. Einstein argued this meant quantum mechanics was incomplete (Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, 1935). Bell showed it could be tested (Bell, 1964), and experiments confirmed it (Aspect et al., 1982), including three loophole-free tests in 2015 (Hensen et al.; Giustina et al.; Shalm et al.). The work won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. Reality is not "local" in the everyday sense.

Measurement

What makes one outcome real?

Quantum mechanics describes a system as a spread of possibilities until it is measured, and then one outcome appears. A century on, physicists still disagree about what a measurement is and why it has that effect; the main interpretations offer incompatible pictures of reality (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Measurement in Quantum Theory").

Consciousness

Why is there experience at all?

Neuroscience can map which brain activity accompanies which experience. It cannot yet explain why any physical process is accompanied by experience in the first place: the "hard problem" of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995). A material account explains what the brain does, not why there is someone there.

What the gaps add up to

Science is incomplete, and seriously so.

Humanity has a habit: in almost every era, it has felt close to having figured everything out. At the end of the nineteenth century, many physicists believed the foundations of their subject were essentially complete. Within a few years came the quantum (1900) and relativity (1905), and the foundations were rebuilt. We are very likely in the same position today, and further from the full picture than it feels.

Put the list together and the picture is plain. Science cannot say what most of the universe is made of. It cannot reconcile its two best theories. It cannot say what gravity is, what came before the Big Bang, why the laws are what they are, why mathematics describes them, what life is, how it began, or why there is experience at all. These are not small gaps at the edge of knowledge. They sit at the foundations, under everything else we think we know.

The Holistic Model of Reality (HMR) proposes a model far from where mainstream thinking is today. It requires changes at the foundations, chiefly two: that physical reality emerges from Universal Intelligence (UI), and that fascia is the body's primary system for health. Those two changes move the model of reality from the mainstream to something new, and they open a green field for many of the answers science is still looking for.

A materialist account of reality is not a finished picture with a few details missing. It is a precise description of how physical reality behaves, with no agreed account of what that reality is.

There is an irony here. Many people who hold a materialist view are quite sure about the deepest questions: what is sane and what is insane, what is real and what is not. Their own framework cannot say what consciousness is, what a measurement does, or where sanity ends. A view that cannot explain the mind is being used with great confidence to rule on which minds are sound. That confidence is a belief, not a finding.

That is why a holistic model of reality is needed today: one that keeps everything science has established and also offers an account of the foundations science leaves open. Once the full picture is understood, the questions that feel unanswerable become answerable: what life is, what death is, what to do with our day, and what right and wrong behavior are. For readers who trust science

If you value reason, these gaps are a question for you.

Many people who think of themselves as scientific, rational and guided by values hold a materialist view of reality without ever having examined its foundations.

Being scientific means believing in proportion to the evidence. Being rational means noticing when a view no longer makes sense. Being values-driven means caring what our beliefs do to other people. Taken seriously, those three commitments lead to two honest questions:

Do I keep believing in something that, at its foundations, does not make sense?

What harm do I cause if I stay comfortable in it?

The harm is not abstract. A view of reality decides what we think a person is, what we think is possible for a body, what we treat and what we dismiss, what we teach children, and what we owe each other.

You cannot build values on a reality you do not know

Every value system rests on some picture of what reality is: what a person is, whether life has a purpose, what happens after death, whether anything beyond matter matters. If science cannot say what reality is, then a value system that rests on science alone is resting on an open question.

When science is this incomplete, it is a stretch for anyone to assume they know what follows from it.

What we have seen

In conversation after conversation, people lean on science as if it settled what reality is. But when they look closely at the gaps, few of them actually believe it does. And the less someone believes the materialist picture is complete, the more room, and the more reason, they have to want a holistic model of reality.
