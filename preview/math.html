A new paradigm for mathematics.

Why does the universe obey equations? The physicist Eugene Wigner called the usefulness of mathematics in physics "unreasonable" and said "there is no rational explanation for it" (Wigner, 1960). HMR has one. Mathematics is the ordered pattern of thought within a Universal Intelligence (UI), existing among thoughts without order. Nature is mathematical because it is made of that ordered thought.

The mechanics of thought

One kind of thing, one kind of act.

HMR says reality is Universal Intelligence (UI) thinking. If that is so, there should be a mathematics whose only object is a thought and whose only operation is thinking one. This section builds it from HMR's premises, and marks every line with how firm it is.

It is not finished. It reaches as far as an exact family of rules for how one thought follows another, and an exact account of what those rules cannot do. Nothing here was invented to fill a gap: where the mathematics runs out, the page says open.

Birth

Attend to any set S of existing thoughts. A new thought is born after everything in S and after everything S rests on.

established “attention” is a reading

The laws of birth. Each follows directly from the definition.

  1. Nothing is born into the past. A new thought comes after what it attends to, never before.
  2. Attention completes itself. Attending to a thought includes everything that thought rests on.
  3. Unrelated births commute. If neither of two new thoughts attends to the other, the order they are born in makes no difference to the result.
  4. Related births do not. If the second attends to the first, the order is part of the result.
  5. Nothing is removed. Thoughts and relations only accumulate.
  6. Each birth adds one. That is counting.
Everything can be grown

The rule for what is thought next

Ask four things of any rule that gives the chance of each possible next thought:

  1. Nothing is born into the past.
  2. The chance of reaching a state does not depend on the order in which its unrelated thoughts were born.
  3. The odds between two possible births depend only on the two pasts involved, not on thoughts outside them.
  4. Something is always thought next: the chances add to one.

If, in addition, no possible birth has chance zero, the rule has one form. There are numbers t0 = 1, t1, t2 and so on, none negative. In a state of n thoughts, a birth whose past holds w thoughts, m of them seen directly, has chance

chance = λ(w, m) / λ(n, 0)
λ(w, m) = Σk=m…w C(w − m, k − m) tk

for each distinct past. C(a, b) counts the ways to choose b things from a. This is the classical sequential growth law of Rideout and Sorkin (1999), found in the search for a theory of quantum gravity. established

The simplest case

Let each existing thought be attended to with the same chance p, independently of the others. Then the formula becomes

pm (1 − p)n − w

Start with one thought and p = ½. The second thought comes after the first with chance ½, and beside it with chance ½. That is the whole rule, at its smallest.

The same rule, said as attention

Pick a set S of existing thoughts, a set of k thoughts having weight tk. The new thought is born after S and all that S rests on. Checked by exact computation, on every pattern of up to five thoughts, by the mathematician and again by the referee: it gives the formula on the left. established Reading tk as UI's weighting of a focus of size k: HMR reading

It is a family, not one law. The numbers tk are free, infinitely many of them, and nothing in the four conditions fixes them.

Grow a pattern yourself

This runs the simplest case. Each new thought attends to each existing thought with chance p, and comes after everything those rest on. A line joins a thought to the ones directly beneath it, and time runs upward.

Gold marks a post: a thought that every other thought comes before or after. The slider p is the strength of attraction: low p leaves thoughts apart, high p draws them into a single line. This is the simplest rule of the family, shown to make it concrete. It is not offered as UI's law.

Attraction: liking a thing, and so doing more of it

HMR says UI likes some things and so does more of them, and that thoughts which belong together attract and cluster. Attraction is already inside the simplest rule above, in exact form. Nothing had to be added to get it.

Together is free. Apart is paid for.

plinks × (1 − p)pairs apart

This is the chance of a pattern grown in a given order. A link is a direct relation. A relation that follows from other relations costs nothing, and every pair of thoughts left unrelated costs a factor of 1 − p. So p is a strength of attraction: near 1 it draws thoughts into one line, near 0 it leaves them apart.

established (Rideout and Sorkin, 1999), rechecked here on every history of five thoughts

Attention attracts attention

1 − (1 − p)1 + f

This is the chance that the next thought builds on a given thought, where f is the number of thoughts already resting on it. To build on any of those is to build on it too. When p is small the chance is close to p × (1 + f), in proportion to what the thought has already drawn. That is “liking a thing, and so doing more of it”, with no liking put in.

established as liking: HMR reading

Two thoughts pull as a product

pull(x) × pull(y)

This is the chance that the next thought comes after both of two thoughts whose followings are separate, where each pull is the chance in the card before. A product of two weights is the form of the top line of Newton's law of gravity. The bottom line, distance squared, is absent, because nothing in this rule knows distance.

Liking is the mathematics of energy. Give every pattern a cost:

cost = links × ln(1/p)
  + pairs apart × ln(1/(1 − p))

The chance in the first card is then e−cost. Physics writes attraction the same way: at a fixed temperature a system is found in a state with chance in proportion to e−E/T, where E is the energy of the state, and a force is the slope of E. In the general rule below, the chance of a step is a neutral chance times w(next) / w(now), so −ln w plays the part of energy, and the pull toward a liked pattern is the slope of ln w.

“UI is drawn to what it likes” and “things fall toward lower energy” are one statement in two vocabularies. established as mathematics w as liking: HMR reading

Heat has a place in the same language. The temperature T says how loosely a system follows its energy: very hot, every state is about as likely as counting alone makes it; very cold, only the lowest energy survives. For UI that would be how strictly it follows its liking. HMR reading

What attraction still lacks

Distance. This is attraction between thoughts in a pattern. A force in space needs space, and no rule yet grows it. So there is no inverse-square law here.

Two signs. Electric charges attract and repel. A weight above the neutral chance attracts and one below it repels, but nothing yet says what plays the part of charge.

UI's own liking. The number p is one dial. What UI likes, written as a w, is not known.

The quantum form. With w an ordinary positive number the rule cannot produce entanglement. Physics gets it by making the weight of a history a complex number, and electromagnetism acts on the angle of that number. Whether UI's liking is of that kind: HMR conjecture

What that family cannot do, and the door it leaves open

It is blind to shape

The chance of a birth depends on the new thought's past through two numbers only: its size w, and how many of its thoughts are seen directly, m. The rule can favour a wide or a narrow focus. It cannot favour one shape of past over another with the same two numbers.

established

It cannot be the whole law

An ordinary probability rule that obeys a condition like the third cannot produce the linked outcomes seen in entangled particles, if what is measured can be chosen freely. Experiments do produce them. HMR's “what was one never stops being one” needs more than this family.

No space in the large

For every choice of the numbers, a large pattern grown this way is close to one that has an order in time and no structure in space. No rule of the family has a large-scale limit that resembles flat spacetime in two or more dimensions (Brightwell and Georgiou, 2010).

established

So the four-condition rule is a template, not UI's law. Our judgment is that, within ordinary probability, the condition to give up is the third. HMR asks for that three times: for preferring some patterns, for entanglement, and for space. The other way out is a quantum rule, in which all four conditions take a new form. HMR conjecture

What the other three conditions allow. Fix any rule of the family, with all its numbers positive, as a reference. Then the rules that keep conditions 1, 2 and 4 are exactly

chance = reference chance
  × w(next state) / w(present state)

where w gives every pattern a number that is never negative, w of the void is 1, and w of a pattern is the reference-weighted average of w over the patterns that can follow it. Here w is simply the chance of a pattern under the rule divided by its chance under the reference. It is a change of description, not a restriction, and it changes if the reference is changed. These rules are the order-invariant measures studied by Brightwell and Luczak (2011). established

That is an exact meaning for “a closed system” (Martin, O'Connor, Rideout and Sorkin, 2001). established

Geometry from the first distinction

The first boundary as a sphere: what is proved, and what is assumed.

The Science Bridge says that once “me” has a reach, testing its edge in every direction traces a sphere, and with it π. An earlier version of this page called that a proof with one assumption that was “earned, not added”. Checked carefully, it needs three assumptions, and none of them follows from me, not me and the unit alone. Here it is stated correctly.

Given: a flat space of at least two dimensions, with distances that can take any real value; a point O (me); the unit d₀ (the first distance).

Assumption (no hidden structure): the distance-preserving maps that keep O fixed can carry any point at distance d₀ to any other.

Claim: any set of points at distance d₀ from O that is picked out by a rule using only O and d₀ is the whole sphere. In two dimensions its length is 2π d₀.

Proof: a distance-preserving map that keeps O fixed keeps every statement written with O and distance true, so it carries the chosen set onto itself. The chosen set is then made of whole orbits of those maps, and by the assumption the sphere is a single orbit. ∎ established

The fork: what is the first shape?

If the only distance that exists is the unit, the edge of “me” can be one of three things. They differ in how the points on the edge relate to each other.

Sphere

Points on the edge sit at every distance from each other, from almost nothing up to two units straight across. That needs distances which repeating the unit cannot supply, such as √2.

Simplex

Every point is one unit from every other. Two points make a line, three a triangle, four a pyramid, and each further point needs a new dimension. There is no edge to measure and no π.

Fan

Every edge point is one unit from me (gold), and the edge points relate to each other only through me. Any two are then two units apart: across, through the centre.

HMR's own mechanics picks the fan. Thoughts relate only by “came before”, and three or more thoughts cannot all be one direct step from each other in such an order. established The edge thoughts are then unrelated to one another, which is what the mechanics calls being apart in space. The links from me to them are the inner face of the boundary, and whatever later comes to rest on them is its outer face. Only the one unit exists, and every other distance is a count of it. exact in HMR

No edge thought is special: shuffle them in any way and the fan is unchanged, which is the first form of “the same in every direction”. A sphere and π can exist only as limits over very many thoughts. Whether the pattern grows round is the same open question as whether it grows space. open

The meaning of π

π: the unlimited, inside the first limit.

π is the ratio of a circle's edge to its width. Three facts about it matter to HMR.

It is forced. No one chooses π. In flat space, any edge drawn evenly around a point produces it, at every size. It also appears with no geometry at all, from counting two-way choices (above). In HMR it is the signature of the first boundary, the edge of "me".

It never ends. π is transcendental: its digits never stop and never repeat (Lindemann, 1882, proved it is not the root of any polynomial with whole-number coefficients). The first and simplest limit, a single unit distance tested in every direction, contains a number that cannot be written down completely. In HMR's reading, that is the trace of phase 1: the unlimited is still present inside the first limited thing.

It is everywhere. π appears wherever something spreads evenly from a point: in every wave and vibration, in the law of gravity and the law of electric force (the 4π of a sphere's surface), in Einstein's equations (8π), in quantum mechanics (ħ = h/2π), and in the bell curve of chance. HMR reads every appearance as a trace of the original boundary, carried into everything built on it.

One line, five constants

eiπ + 1 = 0

Euler's identity joins the five most fundamental numbers in mathematics: 0, 1, π, e and i. In HMR's terms it is a single sentence about the beginning: nothing (0), the first unit (1), the first boundary (π), growth (e), and a new independent direction (i, the square root of −1, which turns a line into a plane).

What π could translate to

If π is the ratio between reach and boundary, it is also a measure of how much "me" there is for each step of reach: the edge of self grows π times faster than its width. HMR proposes that this ratio, between what a being can reach and the boundary it must hold, is a basic quantity of every self, from the first sphere to a living body.

The catalytic moment

The mathematics of the Big Bang.

The Science Bridge says the Big Bang was the moment UI's first model of itself became complete. An earlier version of this section said more than the mathematics supports. Below is what holds, point by point, and what would have to be shown for the rest.

In the story

UI asked what it is. To answer, it imagined what it is not, measured the gap, tested its own edges in every direction, and worked out how anything inside could relate to anything else. The moment that picture was complete, it could not stay still: a complete picture of oneself is a thought that keeps producing new thoughts. That was the Big Bang. Much later, parts of the universe began to picture themselves from within. That was life.

1. A complete self-model

Let S be what UI is, and F the act of modeling it. The model is complete when the picture and the thing agree:

S* = F(S*)

For the natural choices of F (one more; a pair; any finite grouping; one birth) a smallest solution exists, and in every case it is not finite: n = n + 1, n = 1 + n² and n = 2ⁿ have no solutions. So if closure is this equation, closure is not a finite event. established

Lawvere's fixed-point theorem (Lawvere, 1969) does not say that self-reference always produces fixed points, as this page once did. It says: if a system could represent every way of sorting its own thoughts, then every operation on the values would have a fixed point. For two values, me and not me, swapping them has none. So no complete two-valued self-model exists. That leaves three ways out: the self-model is never total, which agrees with HMR's “UI is not all-knowing”; or there is a third value, undecided, a formal home for “unsure”; or the fixed point is an alternation in time. established That closure forces one of the three: HMR conjecture

2. What does exist: a post

The growth rule above has an exact notion of a closed system, a thought that everything earlier comes before and everything later comes after. Reading the Big Bang as such a moment is an HMR conjecture. It would be confirmed by a growth rule, outside the classical family, whose post is followed by a hot, expanding universe of three space dimensions. No such rule is known. Closed, exactly ↑

3. After closure

This page once said that relations multiply exponentially once a system is closed, and that this is inflation. The count does not support it. Among n parts there are n(n − 1)/2 pairs, which grows as the square. The 2ⁿ groupings are possibilities, not relations forming in time. Exponential growth in time needs every part to produce its own offspring in parallel, and no known rule supplies that. In the one rule we simulated, the width of the pattern rises in a burst after an origin and then stays constant, and the volume of a thought's past grows at a steady rate for only five to seven e-folds (growth by the factor e) before turning linear. That is not inflation. “Inflation is growth after closure” stands as an HMR conjecture with no support at present.

The Millennium Prize problems

Seven famous problems, reframed.

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven of the hardest open problems in mathematics and offered a million dollars for each (Clay Mathematics Institute). One has been solved. If HMR is right, mathematics is UI's ordered thought, and that changes what each problem is really asking. Below, each problem as mathematics frames it, and as HMR reframes it, with the answer HMR expects.

Why can HMR say anything here when mathematicians cannot yet? Because it takes a position on what mathematics is: the ordered thought of UI, built up from a first unit. A position like that implies which way certain questions should fall, in the way a physical theory implies which experiments should succeed. It does not supply the proof. Each problem below is marked with how far HMR actually gets.

To be exact about what this is: these are reframings and expected answers, not proofs. A Clay prize requires a complete proof, published and accepted by mathematicians. An expected answer is a direction, and it is worth something only if it helps someone find the proof.

Riemann hypothesis

Open since 1859

As mathematics frames it

The prime numbers look scattered, but their distribution is governed by the zeros of one function, the zeta function. Are all of its meaningful zeros on a single straight line?

As HMR reframes it

Primes are the units of counting that cannot be built from smaller repetitions: the irreducible thoughts of arithmetic. The question asks whether their seeming disorder is governed by perfect balance.

Expected answer: yes. In HMR, order underlies every apparent disorder that arises within ordered thought; the line is that balance.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Expectation only. HMR predicts which way the answer falls; it has no proof and no new method toward one.

P versus NP

Posed 1971

As mathematics frames it

If an answer can be checked quickly, can it always be found quickly?

As HMR reframes it

Checking follows an ordered path from a given answer. Finding means searching among possibilities with no order yet, the condition of phase 1. The question asks whether order can always be produced as cheaply as it can be recognized.

Expected answer: no, P is not NP. In HMR, imposing order on the unordered is the costly step; UI's whole history is that cost.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Expectation only. HMR gives a reason to expect the answer; a proof must be built inside computer science.

Navier-Stokes

Smoothness of fluid flow

As mathematics frames it

Do the equations of fluid flow, in three dimensions, always have smooth solutions, or can a flow develop an infinite spike in finite time?

As HMR reframes it

The equations treat a fluid as infinitely divisible. In HMR, distance has a first unit, so no physical flow can concentrate below it. The physical answer is clear; the mathematical question is whether the idealized continuum keeps the same order.

Expected answer: smooth solutions exist. The idealization inherits the stability of the reality it describes.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Partial. For real fluids HMR has an answer: a first unit of distance forbids an infinite spike. The prize problem is about the idealized equations, and that remains open.

Yang-Mills and the mass gap

Quantum fields

As mathematics frames it

Prove that the quantum theory of the forces inside atoms exists rigorously, and that its lightest particle has a mass greater than zero.

As HMR reframes it

A gap above nothing is the same idea as a first unit: you cannot have an arbitrarily small amount of something that exists. The question asks whether that minimum is built into the theory.

Expected answer: yes, the theory exists and the gap is real. In HMR, every quantity that exists has a smallest step.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Partial. HMR explains why a gap should exist (every real quantity has a smallest step). Showing it in the mathematics of the theory remains open.

Hodge conjecture

Geometry and algebra

As mathematics frames it

In a large class of shapes, are certain natural features always built from pieces defined by simple equations?

As HMR reframes it

It asks whether the shape of a space is always made of the simplest ordered parts, the way HMR says every structure is built from the original thought bases: point, line, plane and ratio.

Expected answer: yes. In HMR, nothing real is built from anything other than ordered parts.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Expectation only. A direction, not an argument a geometer could use yet.

Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer

Number theory

As mathematics frames it

For a family of curves, is the number of their whole-number-ratio solutions determined by the behavior of a related function at one point?

As HMR reframes it

It asks whether how much order a curve contains, how many exact solutions it has, always shows itself in a single measurable signature.

Expected answer: yes. In HMR, order always leaves a trace that can be read.

If HMR is true, do we have the answer? Expectation only. A direction, not an argument a number theorist could use yet.

Poincaré conjecture

Solved, 2003

As mathematics frames it

Is every closed three-dimensional space without holes a sphere? Grigori Perelman proved it is, and declined the prize.

As HMR reframes it

The first boundary of "me", tested in every direction with nothing to break its symmetry, is a sphere (above).

Answer: yes, as proved. HMR reads it as the deepest of the seven, because it describes the first shape.
