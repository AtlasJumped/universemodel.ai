Simulating reality

A working model of physical reality, with a perfect human inside it

One goal of this work is to model physical reality with a perfect human tensegrity inside it, then change the conditions of that simulation and watch what follows, from health to disease and back again. We are reverse engineering disease: from the optimal state and from every non-optimal state, we walk back how each non-optimal state came to exist.

This is possible now. Technology is advancing very fast, and humanity's innovations have given us extraordinary computing power, AI, and data. It may not be easy, but no one should doubt that it can be done: with today's tools, all it takes is the idea and the will to execute it. That is what we are doing.

Imperfections in this body

The fascia

The fascia here is a few separate systems, each one continuous: every named line on each side of the body, the deep guys of the spine on each side, and the general web region by region. Cut one cord and the whole system it belongs to lets go, from end to end, so the body shifts far from the cut. Break a bone and it snaps where you tap. Tap the head with the bone tool and the skull is crushed. Some damage the rest absorbs with a shift in posture. Other damage brings the body down: a thigh bone, a shin bone, a vertebra in the lower back, the skull, or the front line on both sides. Press Reset to healthy, beside the scenarios, to heal.

Fascial lines

Reading the colors

Tension map: blue relaxed, red strained, dashed slack. The colored lines follow the myofascial chains described in anatomy, with the thoracolumbar fascia and the deep hip and knee rotators added. Densified cables turn red; cables remodeled longer by a twist are dashed orange. Strain on the skin is the stretch of the skin envelope, computed per point. The model includes the skin as a tensioned envelope: like the rubber of a balloon, it pulls any patch that swells outward back toward the body's axis and shares the strain with the patches around it.

How to use it
  • Tap either figure to place an imperfection. Choose Point, Line, Plane or Twist first.
  • Drag to turn the body all the way around; pinch or scroll to zoom. A tap without a drag still picks a point. It stays still unless you switch on Spin.
  • Each imperfection opens an editor: density, friction, mass, volume, and the boundary of its zone. They persist, and stack.
  • Let time pass and the change creeps outward along the fascial lines that leave the zone.
  • The person on the right is drawn on the same computed skeleton. Their posture, symmetry, load, and expression come only from the physics.
This is real math

Simple, but not a cartoon. The skeleton is 45 separate bones, jointed the way a real skeleton is: a ball at each hip and shoulder and between the vertebrae, a hinge at each knee, ankle, and elbow. Nothing in it holds itself up. About 700 tension-only fascia cables do that, against real gravity, which pulls on every one of the 190 points all the time. The cables are drawn taut: the lines rest 1.25 percent shorter than the distance they span and the deep guys of the spine 2 percent shorter, which is close to the most this model can carry before the healthy body folds forward at the chest. Every tap truly redistributes force, and the posture you see is the balance the physics finds. Nothing is animated by hand.

The cables are grouped into a few separate systems: nine named lines on each side, the deep guys of the spine on the left, the right, and the midline, and seven regions of general web. A cut anywhere releases the whole system, as a cut rope carries no tension anywhere along its length. So one cut shifts the posture, and cutting enough, or breaking a bone that carries weight, makes the body really fall. Fascia slides along its lines toward even tension unless friction holds it.

What is simplified: the neck and chest vertebrae are grouped in pairs, the ribs are six pairs on a springy cartilage front, the pelvis is held as one ring by its ligaments, the wrist does not bend, and the feet are planted. Treating a whole line as one band that lets go at once is the strongest version of that idea; real fascia is also tied down along its length. Nothing here contracts. The model stands on passive tension alone, which a living body cannot do: real standing also takes active muscle.

Where the model is going

This is a very simplified tensegrity. The back-end model will grow in fidelity, every version true to the science and still fun to use: as close to the molecule as the data allows, with the real lines of fascia, specific joints and specific bones, and the surface of the skin itself, so that skin tags, hair growth patterns, and areas of excessive sweating can be placed and followed. Why a tensegrity

A mechanic needs the right wrench

Medicine has treated the body as a set of separate systems built like a building: bones as columns, stacked to carry a load from above, with muscles and organs attached. That is a compression structure, and it calls for one toolkit. A tensegrity calls for a completely different one.

In a tensegrity, the rigid parts do not rest on each other. They are held apart and in place by a continuous web of tension. Change the tension anywhere and the whole structure changes shape. Push on one part, and the load spreads through the web instead of down a column.

Think about how a builder fixes a cracked column in a building. They prop the floor above, take the load off the column, and repair or replace it; nothing else in the building needs to change. Fixing a tensegrity is a different job. Every part is held by every other part, so you cannot simply prop one up: you pull parts together and hold them with temporary lines while you build the permanent ones, tuning tensions across the whole structure as you go. If you were repairing a column, you would hire someone who knows columns. If you were repairing a tensegrity, you would hire someone who knows tensegrities. Doctors today are not trained in tensegrities, and human beings are tensegrities. That is a very big problem.

If the body works this way, then many of today's tools are the wrong wrench. Treating a sore knee as a knee, or a stiff neck as a neck, is like tightening one bolt on a suspension bridge without looking at the cables. The mistake is not care or effort; it is the model. If the body is truly a tensegrity, as a growing body of research says (Swanson, 2013; Turvey and Fonseca, 2014; Scarr, 2020; Ingber, 2023), then simply adopting HMR's perspective is an enormous improvement in how we understand health, before any of its larger claims are even considered.

There is a deeper problem. In HMR's view the human body is not yet mature: its tensegrity has not reached the optimized state it is built to reach. So medicine is treating the symptoms of an unfinished body. Modern medicine's mission is the biggest possible toolkit: surgery, protocols, prescriptions, creams, and pills, each a way to attack a symptom. The right tool is an entirely different model of health.

HMR's mission is different. Medicine aims to delay death. The goal of a perfect tensegrity is much closer to the possibility of enlightenment: a body that is whole, takes care of itself, and lets a person live at the full capacity of what they are.

A compression model

  • Bones stack; joints bear the load.
  • Problems are local: fix the part that hurts.
  • Each specialty treats its own system.
  • Soft tissue is packing around the frame.

A tensegrity model

  • Bones float in a continuous web of fascia.
  • Problems can be global: tension travels.
  • The body is studied as one connected structure.
  • Fascia shapes the whole body, and everything inside it.

Two missions

  • Today: a bigger and bigger toolkit of surgery, protocols, prescriptions, creams, and pills, each aimed at a symptom.
  • HMR: a body whose tensegrity is optimized and mature, so the whole system takes care of itself.

What the research says

“An astoundingly wide variety of natural systems, including carbon atoms, water molecules, proteins, viruses, cells, tissues and even humans and other living creatures, are constructed using a common form of architecture known as tensegrity.”

Donald Ingber, cell biologist, Harvard. "The Architecture of Life", Scientific American, January 1998.

“Since its inception, osteopathic medicine has sought to identify the mechanical causes of disease.”

Randel Swanson, opening a review that concludes "the architectural principles of tensegrity can be applied to biological organisms" at every scale. Journal of the American Osteopathic Association, 2013.

Force travels through fascia

A systematic review of dissection and living-body studies found moderate evidence that tension is transmitted across every junction of the superficial back line, from the sole of the foot upward (Krause, Wilke et al., Journal of Anatomy, 2016). Changing the length of one muscle changes the force of its neighbors by up to 20% through the connective tissue between them (Maas and Huijing, 2009).

The whole body senses through it

Researchers have proposed that the body's tensegrity web is the medium of touch and body sense itself: a whole-body structure through which the nervous system feels (Turvey and Fonseca, Journal of Motor Behavior, 2014). Fascia is not passive: it contains contractile cells that tighten the tissue over minutes to months (Schleip and Klingler, 2019).

How far the tensegrity model extends to the whole body is still debated, and some loads in the body are clearly carried in compression. The simulation is how we intend to test it.

What the simulation is for

A baseline. Physical reality and its laws, with an ideal adult human body, male and female, built from real measurements with real limits.

Disease, forwards. Change the conditions: densify fascia in one place, alter a constraint of that world, and let the physics show what happens to the body's shape, its vessels, its nerves, and its organs.

Disease, backwards. Start from the measured shape of a real condition and search for the imperfections that would produce it.

Ways back. Test practices from the ancient traditions and from modern science, from the outside in and from the inside out, and build fascia-first protocols from what the physics shows.

How imperfections are modeled

Every imperfection is placed in the ideal body at a point in space and a moment in time, as a point, a line or a plane of imperfection.

Each has a boundary. Inside it are its local qualities: densified tissue, and whatever runs through it, such as vessels and nerves, each with its own tolerance to compression and stretch. Outside it are the downstream effects on the rest of the body.

In the model above each imperfection carries five numbers: a relative density (shorter, stiffer fascia), its effect on friction (how freely fascia slides through it), a relative mass, a relative volume, and the radius of its boundary. A twist remodels the tissue that crosses a joint.

Boundaries are not smooth. Effects travel along the tissues that pass through an imperfection, especially vessels, so the local zone extends irregularly beyond a simple outline. We call this creep.

How we are building it

From a test model to a measured human

The model above is a test. The full simulation is in a development environment, built from the data in our library layer by layer, so that every dimension and every tissue property comes from a measurement. How every force, tissue, and imperfection is modeled is set out in full on Methods.

1 · The scaffold

79,224 cross-sections

The Visible Human male and female, sectioned head to foot, give the true position of every bone, muscle, organ, vessel, and layer of fascia in two real bodies.

2 · The materials

Measured mechanics

Published tests of tendon, ligament, fascia, and muscle give each tissue its stiffness, stretch, and limits, with the source kept for every number.

3 · The range

Real bodies

Body surveys, motion capture and thousands of images of elite movers set the range of sizes, proportions, and movement the model must reproduce.

4 · The imperfections

Computer vision

Photographs of people and their symptoms can be measured by our computer vision models for anything we need: distances, angles, asymmetries, and patterns, to compare against what the simulation predicts.

Because the data covers both the ideal and the imperfect, the model can be built very thoroughly: the ideal body from the anatomy and mechanics, and the imperfections from the measured shapes of real conditions. What is in the library →

Tension travels

In a tensegrity, rigid parts never touch; they are held in place only by continuous tension. Change the tension in one place and the whole structure adjusts. That is why, in HMR, a small imperfection can have effects far from where it began.

Tap a node on this simpler tensegrity to send a pulse of tension through it. Then cut a cable or collapse a strut and watch the whole structure settle into a new shape, slow the motion right down to follow the force, or switch to other everyday tensegrities, such as a tent, to see the same rule at work.

From model to protocols

Once the ideal body exists as a physical model, outside-in approaches (pressure, stretch, vibration, heat, movement) and inside-out approaches (breath, internal locks, posture, attention, hydration) can each be tested against it. The aim is fascia-first protocols grounded in physics, drawing on both the ancient traditions and modern science.

In short

This is a working tensegrity of the human body: bones as struts, and fascia as about 700 tension-only cables in separate systems. Choose a scenario, add an imperfection, cut a cable or break a bone, and the physics computes what the rest of the body does. The results are listed beside the model as they change. It is a first sketch, built by hand, of the model the project is building from measured anatomy.
