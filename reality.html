A new model of reality

Where today's picture of reality runs out.

Modern physics is the most successful description of physical reality humanity has ever had. It is also incomplete, and some of its deepest open questions point beyond a purely material view.

What we use now

Two great theories that do not fit together.

Newton's laws described motion and gravity so well that they guided science for two centuries. Einstein then showed they were an approximation: in general relativity, gravity is the curvature of space and time. At the smallest scales, quantum mechanics describes matter and light with extraordinary precision.

Each theory is confirmed in its own domain. But they rest on different pictures of what reality is, and no one has found a way to combine them into one theory. Where they must meet, they disagree badly: the energy of empty space predicted by quantum theory exceeds what cosmology observes by a factor of roughly 10120, which has been called the worst prediction in physics (Weinberg, 1989).

And the visible universe is a small part of the whole. By the best current measurements, ordinary matter makes up only about 5% of the universe's contents; the rest is attributed to dark matter and dark energy, whose nature is unknown (Planck Collaboration, 2020).

In short

Physics describes the rules of physical reality with great precision. It does not yet have one account of those rules, and it does not say why they exist, what most of the universe is made of, or how experience arises from matter.

What a material view has not explained

Open questions at the foundations.

None of these proves that materialism is wrong. Each is a place where a purely material account of reality has no agreed answer, and where some of the founders of modern physics themselves expected one to be needed.

Entanglement

Connected across any distance

Measure one of two entangled particles and the other's result is correlated with it instantly, however far apart they are. Einstein argued this meant quantum mechanics was incomplete (Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, 1935). Bell showed it could be tested (Bell, 1964), and experiments confirmed it (Aspect et al., 1982), including three loophole-free tests in 2015 (Hensen et al.; Giustina et al.; Shalm et al.). The work won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. Reality is not "local" in the everyday sense.

Measurement

What makes one outcome real?

Quantum mechanics describes a system as a spread of possibilities until it is measured, and then one outcome appears. A century on, physicists still disagree about what a measurement is and why it has that effect; the main interpretations offer incompatible pictures of reality (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Measurement in Quantum Theory").

Consciousness

Why is there experience at all?

Neuroscience can map which brain activity accompanies which experience. It cannot yet explain why any physical process is accompanied by experience in the first place: the "hard problem" of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995). A material account explains what the brain does, not why there is someone there.

One account, from before the Big Bang to today.

The HMR proposes that physical reality emerges from Universal Intelligence (UI), a non-physical intelligence whose changes over time trend toward greater coherence. In their words

Great scientists who saw more to reality than matter alone.

Each, in their own way, saw more to reality than matter alone. You may have studied their science, but not what they truly believed.

“I regard consciousness as fundamental. I regard matter as derivative from consciousness.”

Max Planck, physicist, founder of quantum theory. Interview in The Observer, 25 January 1931.

“There is no matter as such. All matter originates and exists only by virtue of a force... We must assume behind this force the existence of a conscious and intelligent mind. This mind is the matrix of all matter.”

Max Planck. Lecture "Das Wesen der Materie" (The Nature of Matter), Florence, 1944.

“Everyone who is seriously involved in the pursuit of science becomes convinced that a spirit is manifest in the laws of the Universe, a spirit vastly superior to that of man.”

Albert Einstein, physicist. Letter to a schoolgirl, Phyllis Wright, 24 January 1936.

“Science without religion is lame, religion without science is blind.”

Albert Einstein. "Science and Religion", Conference on Science, Philosophy and Religion, New York, 1941.

“I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of all that exists, but not in a God who concerns himself with the fate and the doings of mankind.”

Albert Einstein. Telegram to Rabbi Herbert Goldstein, April 1929.

“This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being.”

Isaac Newton, physicist and mathematician. Principia, General Scholium (1713), trans. Andrew Motte.

“Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived.”

Baruch Spinoza, philosopher. Ethics (1677), Part I, Proposition 15, trans. R. H. M. Elwes.

“The scientific picture of the real world around me is very deficient. It gives a lot of factual information... but it is ghastly silent about all and sundry that is really near to our heart... It knows nothing of beautiful and ugly, good or bad, God and eternity.”

Erwin Schrödinger, physicist, a founder of quantum mechanics. Nature and the Greeks (1954).

“Consciousness is a singular of which the plural is unknown.”

Erwin Schrödinger. Mind and Matter (1958).

“Atman = Brahman: the personal self equals the omnipresent, all-comprehending eternal self.”

Erwin Schrödinger. What is Life? (1944), Epilogue.

“To put the conclusion crudely, the stuff of the world is mind-stuff.”

Arthur Eddington, astrophysicist. The Nature of the Physical World (1928).

“The universe begins to look more like a great thought than like a great machine. Mind no longer appears to be an accidental intruder into the realm of matter.”

James Jeans, physicist and astronomer. The Mysterious Universe (1930).

“It would be most satisfactory of all if physis and psyche (that is, matter and mind) could be seen as complementary aspects of the same reality.”

Wolfgang Pauli, physicist, Nobel laureate. Essay on Kepler, in The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche, with C. G. Jung (1952).

“It was not possible to formulate the laws of quantum mechanics in a fully consistent way without reference to the consciousness.”

Eugene Wigner, physicist, Nobel laureate. "Remarks on the Mind-Body Question" (1961).

Freeman Dyson, physicist and mathematician. Disturbing the Universe (1979).

“I am convinced of the afterlife, independent of theology.”
