Methods

How the model is built, and how we try not to fool ourselves

This page is for readers who want the method laid out the way a scientist would: what the model is made of, where its numbers come from, how it is computed, how it is tested, and what it cannot yet do.

The pipeline, end to end

1. CollectAnatomy, imaging, and tissue mechanics from the literature and open datasets.
2. IndexLocal AI models read, sort, and link every source, including evidence against the model.
3. BuildThe ideal male and female bodies, layer by layer, from measured values.
4. PerturbAdd imperfections, sampled blind to any disease label.
5. SimulateLet each body settle to the shape its physics produces.
6. CompareMatch outcomes to real measurements, after the fact, with controls.

The approach: an ideal human biotensegrity

We are building, from real data, a model of an ideal adult body in male and female form. It is assembled in layers that can be switched on and off, so the contribution of each layer can be measured on its own.

Layer 1

Skeleton

Every bone as its own exact 3D object, placed at its true distance from every other. On its own, a skeleton has no limits: the bones could drift apart freely.

Layer 2

Connective tissue

The continuous web of connective tissue around and between the bones. Adding it creates the first real limits, and the tension that holds the body together.

Layer 3

Everything inside

Organs, vessels, nerves, skin, and hair follicles, with their shapes, depths and spacing. Each one adds new constraints and new mechanics.

Layer 4

Mechanics

Shear, stress, tension, friction, and stretch for every tissue, from molecules to whole limbs, using measured values and physical limits.

Layer 5

Movement

A complete range of motion, checked against yoga, contortion and extreme athletic movement, so the model can move the way the best human bodies do.

Layer 6

Experience

How the nervous system reads the body's physics as sensation, and how physical and mental health interact. Kept as separate, switchable layers.

Layer 7

The external environment

Everything outside the body that pushes on it: gravity and its direction, the ground, footwear, clothing and compression, temperature, water and humidity on the skin, carried loads, and the tools of the protocol.

Around every layer

Starting conditions and limits

Where each run begins (geometry from imaging, prestress, hydration, temperature, age, sex, posture) and the limits it must respect: the strains at which each tissue is damaged, joint ranges, and the bounds of every measured value.

What "ideal" means, operationally. Starting from measured bodies, the model asks repeatedly what shape is mechanically better: tension shared more evenly across the web, lower shear and friction between layers, less strain on vessels and nerves, more range of motion. The ideal is the shape where no such improvement remains, within measured tissue limits. It is defined by an optimization, not by an artist.

The grain of the model

Built cell by cell

The ideal would be to compute the whole body atom by atom. No computer can do that, and molecule by molecule is most likely out of reach too. So the target is the cell.

The model's unit is a cell: one small piece of tissue with a type, a 3D shape, and a surface. On that surface are the points where it can join its neighbors or let go of them. Each cell carries the properties it needs to pass on force, pressure, and heat, and a set amount of material and energy, with a healthy baseline.

Everything starts from a master cell that can become any type, the way a stem cell can. Each tissue is a derived type with its own properties.

The 3D shape of a human is filled with master cells. Imaging then tells each one which tissue it is, at that place in the body. The layers above describe what gets assigned, and this is how it is assigned. How fine the cells can be depends on the computing available. This is the engineering help we are asking for.

Data sources

Anatomy. The U.S. National Library of Medicine's Visible Human male and female, section by section; labeled CT and MRI datasets; open 3D anatomy models; classical anatomy texts. The library, in full →

Tissue properties. Published measurements of stiffness, strength, thickness, hydration, sliding, and viscoelastic behavior for bone, connective tissue (often called fascia), ligament, tendon, muscle, skin, and vessels, each with its source and its uncertainty recorded.

Ranges of motion. Measured joint ranges from the clinical literature, checked against what trained yoga practitioners, dancers, and contortionists can actually do, so the model is not limited by average bodies.

Comparison data. Imaging, body shape, and clinical measurements of people living with specific conditions, held separately and used only at the comparison stage.

Indexing

Everything is indexed with AI models that run on our own hardware, so the collection stays private and any question can be answered from it, with citations, in seconds. The index is built to surface contradicting evidence as readily as supporting evidence.

The physics

The body is modeled as a biotensegrity: bones as compression members, connective tissue as a continuous network that carries tension, with sliding interfaces between layers. Each tension element follows a measured constitutive law: stiff in pull, slack in push, and time-dependent (creep and stress relaxation). The body's shape at any moment is the equilibrium the forces reach; nothing about posture is scripted.

For a tension element between points i and j with rest length L₀ and stiffness k:

F = k · max(0, |xj − xi| − L₀)

Equilibrium is found by relaxing the network until the net force on every point vanishes (within tolerance), with gravity, contact, and volume constraints applied. The full model adds finite-element treatment of continuous sheets and organs, and fluid pressure in compartments.

The full model: every force, in every tissue, at every interface

Every physical effect in the body has to be captured as a force. So the model is built from three complete lists, the forces, the tissues, and the interfaces between tissues, and a law for every combination: how each tissue responds to each force, and how each pair of neighboring tissues pass force between them. This is the beginning of the list, and it will keep growing.

Forces

Every force acting in the body

  • Gravity and inertia in movement
  • Ground reaction and impact
  • Active muscle contraction
  • Passive tension and resting prestress
  • Compression, bending and torsion
  • Shear between and within layers
  • Friction and adhesion at interfaces
  • Hydrostatic and osmotic pressure
  • Blood pressure, as a pulsing wave
  • Breathing pressures in chest and abdomen
  • Surface tension in the lungs
  • Viscous drag of moving fluids
  • Vibration and its transmission
  • Thermal expansion and contraction
  • Electrical effects, including collagen's piezoelectricity
  • Growth, remodeling and contractile cells in connective tissue
  • External forces: tools, clothing, shoes, loads
Tissues

Every tissue type

  • Cortical and trabecular bone; marrow
  • Hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage; discs
  • Ligament, tendon, aponeurosis, retinacula
  • Deep fascia, superficial fascia, loose areolar tissue
  • Epimysium, perimysium, endomysium
  • Skeletal, smooth, and cardiac muscle
  • Visceral fascia, mesentery, organ capsules
  • Organ tissue: lung, liver, gut, kidney and more
  • Arteries, veins, capillaries, and lymphatics
  • Nerves and their sheaths; dura and meninges
  • Adipose tissue
  • Skin: epidermis, dermis, hair follicles
  • Fluids: blood, lymph, interstitial and synovial fluid, the hyaluronan layer
Properties and interfaces

For every tissue, and every pair

  • Density, mass, and volume
  • Stiffness in each direction, and Poisson's ratio
  • Viscoelasticity: creep and stress relaxation
  • Tensile, compressive, shear, and tear strength
  • Permeability to fluid; hydration
  • Thickness, fiber direction, cross-linking
  • Prestress and damping
  • Thermal and electrical properties
  • Rate of growth, damage, and healing
  • Interfaces: bonded, sliding (with a friction coefficient), lubricated, adhered, tethered, or embedded, as when a vessel runs inside connective tissue

The laws that tie them together

Every point obeys the balance of momentum, mass times acceleration equals the sum of forces on it:

ρ ü = ∇·σ + f

Each tissue supplies its own constitutive law, the relation between stress σ and its deformation history, from measured data: fiber-reinforced hyperelastic laws for connective tissue, ligament, and tendon, viscoelastic terms for creep and relaxation, poroelastic flow for fluid moving through tissue (Darcy's law), and flow equations for blood and lymph. Interfaces obey contact and friction laws, with sliding allowed until the shear stress exceeds the friction coefficient times the pressure (τ ≤ μ p). Remodeling laws let tissue slowly change its properties in response to the loads it carries.

Why it is exhaustive

Leave a force out and the model will explain its effects with something else, and be wrong in a way nobody notices. The only safe model is one where every force is present, even the small ones, so the physics, not our assumptions, decides which matter.

The imperfection architecture

An imperfection is a defined object: a geometry (point, line, plane, or region) placed at a location and time, with a boundary, carrying changed tissue properties such as density, friction, adhesion, mass, volume, pressure, stiffness, strength, hydration, fiber direction, cross-linking, and resting tension, and with its effects outside the boundary computed, not assumed. The full property list →

Combinations are sampled with space-filling designs, so the space of possible bodies is covered evenly rather than clustered around what we expect, and the sampler never sees a disease label.

Testing, with safeguards

Blinded design

Imperfections are sampled without reference to any condition. The model never sees what a disease is supposed to look like.

Pre-registered predictions

What we expect to see is written down and dated before any comparison is made.

Held-out data

Part of the real data is set aside, never used while building, and used once at the end.

Negative controls

Random, shuffled, and physically meaningless imperfections are run too. If they match disease as well as real ones, the result means nothing.

Sensitivity analysis

Every tissue value is varied across its measured uncertainty, to check that a result does not hang on one uncertain number.

Failures reported

Conditions the model cannot reproduce are published alongside those it can.

The first test is the densification hypothesis: densify the connective tissue in chosen places in the ideal body and see whether the shapes and strains that result match real conditions. It fails wherever the effects stay local or do not match.

The first human trial

From the simulation to people

The simulation tests HMR's picture of the body in physics. The claim that connective tissue care changes health also has to be tested in people. We have designed the first trial in full.

The design

One thousand United States veterans live for one year on a campus in Southern California and follow the HMR Connective Tissue Health Protocol each day: three short showers, at least 30 minutes of immersion, stretching, and movement.

The measures

Knee function and chronic pain on published, validated scales, at baseline and every three months. Daily records of how much of the protocol each person did, so dose can be compared with outcome. The community itself is a second hypothesis.

The safeguards

Hypotheses registered before the first participant arrives, a comparison group, assessors who do not know each participant's dose, and failures reported. The trial needs a principal investigator and the approval of an Institutional Review Board before it can begin.

It is a design, not a running study. Nothing has been funded or approved. The full plan covers the hypotheses, the facility, a day in the life, the budget, and the ethics.

Compute

The simulations and the library indexing run on our own workstation:

Graphics (GPUs)2 × NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 (ASUS TUF Gaming), Blackwell architecture, 32 GB of GDDR7 memory each, 64 GB in total. Used for simulation and for the local AI models that index the library.
ProcessorAMD Ryzen Threadripper 9970X: 32 cores, 64 threads, Zen 5, up to 5.49 GHz.
Memory128 GB of DDR5 ECC RAM (4 × 32 GB, Kingston, 4800 MT/s). ECC corrects memory errors, which matters for long runs.
Fast storage10 TB of NVMe solid-state drives: Samsung 9100 PRO 2 TB (system), Samsung 9100 PRO 4 TB (working data: indexes, embeddings, current runs) and a T-FORCE 4 TB (disposable scratch).
Archive storage2 × 16 TB hard drives as a mirror: every file is written to both, so one drive can fail without losing anything.
File systemZFS on every drive, which checksums each block so silent corruption is detected, and compresses the archive.
TotalAbout 42 TB of raw capacity; about 26 TB usable, because the archive stores everything twice.
Board and powerASUS Pro WS TRX50-SAGE WIFI workstation board; SuperFlower 1600 W 80+ Titanium power supply.

The storage is organized for this process: the working set (the search indexes, embeddings, the models in use, and the current simulation runs) sits on the fastest drives, and the raw archive (papers, datasets, imaging, and media) is stored by source, with manifests and catalogs, so a run can trace each number back to where it came from. Each simulated body is independent of the others, so the campaign divides cleanly across both GPUs, and the method scales to larger clusters without changing. Compute is a constraint, but not a large one.

Who else is doing this

Others model the body from physics. None we found start from connective tissue

We are not the first to simulate the human body with physics, and we build on that work. What we have not found is anyone modeling the whole body as one connective tissue biotensegrity, measured against an ideal reference. Here is the field as we understand it.

Movement

OpenSim

Open-source software from Stanford, used by thousands of researchers, that models bones as rigid links driven by muscle-tendon actuators to study how people move. Connective tissue is not modeled as a continuous web.

opensim.stanford.edu → · the 2018 paper →

One organ, in depth

The Living Heart Project

Dassault Systèmes and partners built a detailed physics simulation of a beating human heart, used to test devices and treatments. It shows what a full physics model of one organ takes.

Living Heart Project →

Muscle and control

MyoSim and MyoSuite

Fast musculoskeletal models in the MuJoCo physics engine, built so that learning algorithms can practice controlling a simulated body.

MyoSim paper →

Connective tissue, in theory

Biotensegrity and connective tissue chains

Stephen Levin's biotensegrity, Thomas Myers' myofascial chains and related ideas describe the body as a tension network. A 2020 review sets these theoretical models side by side; they are concepts and anatomy, not running whole-body simulations.

Regional tensegrity models

Where this project differs

The whole web, against an ideal

Three things together: the whole body at once; connective tissue as the continuous load-bearing web, not an add-on to muscles; and an ideal reference body, so that every imperfection is a measured distance from it. If you know of work we have missed, tell us.

Known limits

  • Tissue measurements vary widely between studies, between people, and between living and preserved tissue.
  • Some of the data on connective tissue's long-term behavior comes from animal tissue or short experiments. That points to a larger problem: humanity is not studying connective tissue nearly enough.
  • A model with many parameters can fit almost anything. The safeguards above exist because of this, and results that survive them will still need independent replication.
  • The model describes mechanics. Where HMR proposes more, for example about the mind or about Universal Intelligence, those claims are labeled separately as hypotheses.
In short

The approach is an ideal human biotensegrity, built in layers from published measurements of bone, connective tissue, and other tissue up to a whole body, and computed with stated physics that anyone can inspect. Imperfections are then added to it one at a time. Predictions are registered before the results are seen and tested with safeguards.
