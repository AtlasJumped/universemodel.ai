A new paradigm for mathematics.

Why does the universe obey equations? The physicist Eugene Wigner called the usefulness of mathematics in physics "unreasonable" and said "there is no rational explanation for it" (Wigner, 1960). The HMR has one. Mathematics is the ordered pattern of thought within a Universal Intelligence (UI), existing among thoughts without order. Nature is mathematical because it is made of that ordered thought.

What numbers mean

Reading mathematics from the inside.

If mathematics is ordered thought, then its basic objects are not abstractions floating free of the world. Each one is a stage in UI coming to understand "me" and "not me" (Science Bridge).

Me and not me. Two is the smallest number of things that can differ, and difference is what lets anything be known.

Counting is repetition

With one limit in place, the only thing UI can do is repeat it. Counting is that repetition, made explicit: 1, 1+1, 1+1+1.

A pattern is a stable thought

A pattern is a thought that, repeated, gives the same result. Stability is what lets many beings share one world, which is why physics is a set of patterns.

A proof is unbroken order

A proof is a path of thought from one ordered statement to another that never leaves order. That is why a proof, once found, holds for everyone: it is the shared structure of the one intelligence.

A proof from the Science Bridge

Why the first boundary must be a sphere.

The Science Bridge says that once "me" has a reach, the unit distance, testing its edge in every direction traces a sphere, and with it π. Here is the argument written as a proof, with its one assumption named.

Given: a reference point O (me) and a unit distance d₀ (the first distance). Nothing else exists.

Assumption (no hidden structure): the directions away from O carry no labels, marks or preferences. Any two directions are interchangeable.

  1. The edge of "me" is the set B of points where not-me begins: the points that the one available limit, d₀, picks out.
  2. Any rule for choosing B can only use what exists: O and d₀. A rule that treated one direction differently from another would need information that distinguishes them, and by the assumption there is none.
  3. So B must be unchanged by every rearrangement of directions that keeps O and d₀ fixed. In mathematical terms, B is invariant under all rotations about O.
  4. The rotations about a point can carry any direction to any other. So if B contains one point at distance d₀, it contains every point at distance d₀.
  5. Therefore B is the set of all points at distance d₀ from O: a circle in two independent directions, a sphere in three.
  6. For any such circle, the ratio of its length to its diameter is the same number, whatever the size: π. The boundary forces π. ∎

Why the assumption is earned, not added. A world in which directions were marked, a grid say, would need something to place the marks, and with only O and d₀ in existence there is nothing to do it. The same reasoning is used throughout physics, where it is called Curie's principle: a symmetry present in the causes appears in the effects. The one thing the proof still takes as given is that directions form a continuum rather than a finite set; a finite set would again need something to choose which directions count.

Repeat, and learn

With nothing else in existence, once the limit is in place, all UI can do is repeat it and see what happens. Each repetition produces new data, and from that data the whole of reality can extrapolate the next step. The sphere is the first result of that process. Dimensions, then space and time, are the next.

The same result, later in history

In 2003 Grigori Perelman proved the Poincaré conjecture, one of the Millennium Prize problems: any closed three-dimensional space without holes or edges is, in shape, a sphere. In the HMR's reading it is the same truth seen from far downstream: a closed boundary, with nothing to break its symmetry, is a sphere. The Millennium problems →

The meaning of π

π: the unlimited, inside the first limit.

π is the ratio of a circle's edge to its width. Three facts about it matter to the HMR.

It is forced. No one chooses π. Any edge drawn evenly around a point produces it, at every size. In the HMR it is the signature of the first boundary, the edge of "me".

It never ends. π is transcendental: its digits never stop and never repeat (Lindemann, 1882, proved it is not the root of any polynomial with whole-number coefficients). The first and simplest limit, a single unit distance tested in every direction, contains a number that cannot be written down completely. In the HMR's reading, that is the trace of phase 1: the unlimited is still present inside the first limited thing.

It is everywhere. π appears wherever something spreads evenly from a point: in every wave and vibration, in the law of gravity and the law of electric force (the 4π of a sphere's surface), in Einstein's equations (8π), in quantum mechanics (ħ = h/2π), and in the bell curve of chance. The HMR reads every appearance as a trace of the original boundary, carried into everything built on it.

One line, five constants

eiπ + 1 = 0

Euler's identity joins the five most fundamental numbers in mathematics: 0, 1, π, e and i. In the HMR's terms it is a single sentence about the beginning: nothing (0), the first unit (1), the first boundary (π), growth (e), and a new independent direction (i, the square root of −1, which turns a line into a plane).

What π could translate to

If π is the ratio between reach and boundary, it is also a measure of how much "me" there is for each step of reach: the edge of self grows π times faster than its width. The HMR proposes that this ratio, between what a being can reach and the boundary it must hold, is a basic quantity of every self, from the first sphere to a living body.

The catalytic moment

The mathematics of the Big Bang.

The Science Bridge says the Big Bang was the moment UI's first model of itself became complete. Here is how that can be said in mathematics. These are the HMR's proposals, built from standard ideas.

1. A self-model reaches a fixed point. Let S be what UI is, and F the act of modeling it: F(S) is UI's picture of itself. The model is complete when the picture and the thing agree:

S* = F(S*)

Mathematics knows such fixed points well. Self-reference always produces them, and Lawvere's fixed-point theorem shows why (Lawvere, 1969). In the HMR, the fixed point is reached when a point, a not-point, the distance between them, the boundary tested in every direction and the rules relating everything inside it all describe one another completely.

2. A closed system grows explosively. Once the system is closed, every new thought is a new relation among parts it already has. With n parts there are n(n−1)/2 pairs and 2n possible groupings, so relations multiply far faster than parts. Growth by doubling is exponential, and the earliest universe, in the standard theory of inflation, also grew exponentially, its size scaling as a(t) ∝ eHt. The HMR proposes they are the same event described twice.

3. Life is the same pattern, turned inward. After the explosion, the universe models itself again. The HMR proposes a second catalytic event, an inversion: subsystems that contain a working model of themselves, and so can repair and copy themselves. That is a recognized definition of life in theoretical biology, as self-producing (autopoietic) systems, and John von Neumann showed the logic of a machine that holds its own description and builds itself from it.

In the story

UI asked what it is. To answer, it imagined what it is not, measured the gap, tested its own edges in every direction, and worked out how anything inside could relate to anything else. The moment that picture was complete, it could not stay still: a complete picture of oneself is a thought that keeps producing new thoughts. That was the Big Bang. Much later, parts of the universe began to picture themselves from within. That was life.

Open work

To turn these proposals into a theory: define F precisely; show that its fixed point is unique; derive the rate of growth after closure and compare it with the measured properties of inflation. That is a program, and we name it as one.

The Millennium Prize problems

Seven famous problems, reframed.

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven of the hardest open problems in mathematics and offered a million dollars for each (Clay Mathematics Institute). One has been solved. If the HMR is right, mathematics is UI's ordered thought, and that changes what each problem is really asking. Below, each problem as mathematics frames it, and as the HMR reframes it, with the answer the HMR expects.

To be exact about what this is: these are reframings and expected answers, not proofs. A Clay prize requires a complete proof, published and accepted by mathematicians. An expected answer is a direction, and it is worth something only if it helps someone find the proof.

Riemann hypothesis

Open since 1859

As mathematics frames it

The prime numbers look scattered, but their distribution is governed by the zeros of one function, the zeta function. Are all of its meaningful zeros on a single straight line?

As the HMR reframes it

Primes are the units of counting that cannot be built from smaller repetitions: the irreducible thoughts of arithmetic. The question asks whether their seeming disorder is governed by perfect balance.

Expected answer: yes. In the HMR, order underlies every apparent disorder that arises within ordered thought; the line is that balance.

P versus NP

Posed 1971

As mathematics frames it

If an answer can be checked quickly, can it always be found quickly?

As the HMR reframes it

Checking follows an ordered path from a given answer. Finding means searching among possibilities with no order yet, the condition of phase 1. The question asks whether order can always be produced as cheaply as it can be recognized.

Expected answer: no, P is not NP. In the HMR, imposing order on the unordered is the costly step; UI's whole history is that cost.

Navier-Stokes

Smoothness of fluid flow

As mathematics frames it

Do the equations of fluid flow, in three dimensions, always have smooth solutions, or can a flow develop an infinite spike in finite time?

As the HMR reframes it

The equations treat a fluid as infinitely divisible. In the HMR, distance has a first unit, so no physical flow can concentrate below it. The physical answer is clear; the mathematical question is whether the idealized continuum keeps the same order.

Expected answer: smooth solutions exist. The idealization inherits the stability of the reality it describes.

Yang-Mills and the mass gap

Quantum fields

As mathematics frames it

Prove that the quantum theory of the forces inside atoms exists rigorously, and that its lightest particle has a mass greater than zero.

As the HMR reframes it

A gap above nothing is the same idea as a first unit: you cannot have an arbitrarily small amount of something that exists. The question asks whether that minimum is built into the theory.

Expected answer: yes, the theory exists and the gap is real. In the HMR, every quantity that exists has a smallest step.

Hodge conjecture

Geometry and algebra

As mathematics frames it

In a large class of shapes, are certain natural features always built from pieces defined by simple equations?

As the HMR reframes it

It asks whether the shape of a space is always made of the simplest ordered parts, the way the HMR says every structure is built from the original thought bases: point, line, plane and ratio.

Expected answer: yes. In the HMR, nothing real is built from anything other than ordered parts.

Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer

Number theory

As mathematics frames it

For a family of curves, is the number of their whole-number-ratio solutions determined by the behavior of a related function at one point?

As the HMR reframes it

It asks whether how much order a curve contains, how many exact solutions it has, always shows itself in a single measurable signature.

Expected answer: yes. In the HMR, order always leaves a trace that can be read.

Poincaré conjecture

Solved, 2003

As mathematics frames it

Is every closed three-dimensional space without holes a sphere? Grigori Perelman proved it is, and declined the prize.

As the HMR reframes it

The first boundary of "me", tested in every direction with nothing to break its symmetry, is a sphere (above).

Answer: yes, as proved. The HMR reads it as the deepest of the seven, because it describes the first shape.
