Simulating reality

A working model of physical reality, with a perfect human inside it.

The goal is to model physical reality with a perfect human tensegrity inside it, then change the conditions of that world and watch what follows, from health to disease and back again.

In progress The data for the model is being collected now. The interactive figures below are illustrations, not the model itself.

What the simulation is for.

A baseline. Physical reality and its laws, with an ideal adult human body, male and female, built from real measurements with real limits.

Disease, forwards. Change the conditions: densify fascia in one place, alter a constraint of that world, and let the physics show what happens to the body's shape, its vessels, its nerves and its organs.

Disease, backwards. Start from the measured shape of a real condition and search for the imperfections that would produce it.

Ways back. Test practices from the ancient traditions and from modern science, from the outside in and from the inside out, and build fascia-first protocols from what the physics shows.

How imperfections are modeled

Every imperfection is placed in the ideal body at a point in space and a moment in time, as a point, a line or a plane of imperfection.

Each has a boundary. Inside it are its local qualities: densified tissue, and whatever runs through it, such as vessels and nerves, each with its own tolerance to compression and stretch. Outside it are the downstream effects on the rest of the body.

Boundaries are not smooth. Effects travel along the tissues that pass through an imperfection, especially vessels, so the local zone extends irregularly beyond a simple outline. We call this creep.

Try it: a human tensegrity.

Bones are the dark struts; fascia is the web of cables, blue when healthy. Press a node to create an imperfection: the fascia there is densified and stays that way, and the rest of the body responds.

Healthy: a perfect tensegrity at time zero.

Things to try

  • Press the upper chest four or five times and watch the upper back round forward.
  • Choose Line and draw from the throat to the pubis: a tight front line.
  • Choose Plane across the lower back, sacrum and belly.

This is a simple teaching model in two dimensions. The real model will be three-dimensional, built from measured anatomy and tissue mechanics, and run in time.

Why a tensegrity.

In a tensegrity, rigid parts never touch; they are held in place only by continuous tension. Change the tension in one place and the whole structure adjusts. That is why, in the HMR, a small imperfection can have effects far from where it began.

Tap a node on this simpler tensegrity to send a pulse of tension through it.

From model to protocols.

Once the ideal body exists as a physical model, outside-in approaches (pressure, stretch, vibration, heat, movement) and inside-out approaches (breath, internal locks, posture, attention, hydration) can each be tested against it. The aim is fascia-first protocols grounded in physics, drawing on both the ancient traditions and modern science.

Change one thing. Let the physics decide what follows.
