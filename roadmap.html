The road ahead

From a library to a new paradigm for health.

The work moves in stages. Each one depends on the last, and we would rather move carefully than early.

  1. Now

    Collect

    Gather the research, anatomy, measurements and images that the twelve datasets need. Free and open sources first, then the most useful paid ones.

  2. Next

    Index and organize

    Use local AI models on our own hardware to read, sort and connect everything, so any question can be answered from the evidence in seconds, including the evidence against us.

  3. Next

    Model the ideal human body

    Build the male and female reference bodies layer by layer, bones to experience, with real measurements and real limits, and check them against what real bodies can and cannot do. Then run the first test: the densification hypothesis.

  4. Then

    Trace disease

    Add imperfections to the ideal, compare the results with images and measurements of people living with specific conditions, and look for the paths that connect them.

  5. Then

    Test

    Turn the model's predictions into tests that others can run, and publish what holds up and what does not.

  6. Later

    Share and build

    Explain the work openly, to researchers and to the public, and build the partnerships needed to take it further.

How the model will be used.

As a reference. A measured picture of the healthy body against which any body can be compared.

As a map of causes. For a given condition, a traced path from the ideal to the body that has it, pointing to where things first went wrong.

As a test bed. A way to ask, before anyone tries anything on a person, what a change to the body's mechanics would be expected to do.

As a teacher. A body that can be watched moving through every yoga pose and extreme movement, showing what the human design allows.

How we will know if we are wrong

A model of the ideal body makes predictions. If it produces something real bodies show to be impossible, or fails to explain the shapes that conditions actually produce, it is wrong in that place, and we will say so.

That is why the evidence against the model is collected from the very start.

The long-term vision.

If the model holds up, it offers something health care has never had: a clear picture of what full health is, and a way to measure the distance to it. That would change how conditions are diagnosed, how root causes are searched for, and how treatment approaches are designed and tested.

We imagine long-term studies with large groups of volunteers, open education that teaches people how their own bodies work, and affordable tools that make the work accessible everywhere, not only where health care is expensive.

A more hopeful picture of what a human being is, and what a human body can become.

This is the direction, not a promise. Each stage has to earn the next.
