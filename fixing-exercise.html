Fixing exercise

Optimize the tensegrity first. Then exercise.

Most exercise programs assume the body that is training is already sound. In the Holistic Model of Reality (HMR), almost no adult body is: it is an imperfect tensegrity, and an imperfect tensegrity carries force unevenly. Training it harder makes the imperfections stronger.

Research and education, not medical advice. Do not change a treatment or rehabilitation plan without your clinician. Where this page states the HMR's view, it says so; where it cites research, the source is linked.

The problem every program shares

An immature tensegrity transmits force the wrong way.

The human body behaves as a tensegrity: rigid bones held in place by a continuous, pre-tensioned web of fascia (the evidence is on Physical Health). In a well-tuned tensegrity, a load applied anywhere is shared across the whole web. Every cable takes a little, none takes too much, and the structure springs back.

An immature, non-optimized tensegrity is different. Where fascia has become denser, shorter or stuck, those lines carry far more than their share, and the lines around them go slack. Force no longer spreads; it funnels. From a physics standpoint, that is the common problem behind every protocol on this page: the energy of each step, lift or sprint flows through a broken, uneven web, and the same regions are overloaded again and again.

The HMR's view: this uneven flow is why so much exercise produces injury, why gains fade when training stops, and why the body redistributes mass in ways people do not want. The cure is not to stop moving. It is to change the order.

How tension is shared, in plain physics

  • Prestress. Cables are always slightly stretched. That built-in tension is what makes a tensegrity stiff; in living cells, stiffness rises in proportion to prestress (Wang et al., PNAS, 2001).
  • Load sharing. A push at one point changes tension in every cable a little, instead of crushing one column a lot.
  • Concentration. Shorten or stiffen one cable and it becomes a hot spot: the peak tension in the web rises sharply and neighbors go slack.
  • Accommodation. Cut a cable and the structure does not simply fail at that point. It sways, folds and settles into a new, worse shape, and every other part now works harder.

Harmful before optimization

Weightlifting and hypertrophy training

What people believe
Lifting breaks muscle down so it rebuilds bigger and stronger, and that this is what a healthy body looks like.
What it gets right
Strength matters, and resistance training is one of the few exercise interventions shown to reduce sports injuries in trials.
The HMR's view
The picture misses the web. Heavy load travels through fascia that is not optimized, and the force reaches a region that is not ready to hold it: that is where people get hurt. Muscle is built on top of the imperfections and locks them in. When lifting stops, the unoptimized tensegrity redistributes that mass in ways people do not want, and they feel weak and soft again.
“From 1990 to 2007, 25 335 weight training injuries were seen in US emergency departments, correlating to an estimated 970 801 injuries nationwide.” “The most common diagnosis was sprain/strain (46.1%).”Kerr, Collins and Comstock, Am J Sports Med, 2010 (research)
“There was a significant decrease in muscle size following 31-52 weeks of training cessation”Grgic, Int J Environ Res Public Health, 2022, meta-analysis in older adults (research)
Harmful before optimization

Running and jogging

What it gets right
Cardiovascular fitness, time outdoors, and a simple habit almost anyone can start.
The HMR's view
Every stride sends an impact up the body. With an uneven web, that impact is not spread: it is funneled into the same knees, shins, heels and hips, thousands of times an hour. Joints take what the fascia should have shared. Many people dislike running for a reason; the body is reporting the strain.
“The incidence of lower extremity running injuries ranged from 19.4% to 79.3%. The predominant site of these injuries was the knee.”van Gent et al., Br J Sports Med, 2007, systematic review (research)
“The meta-analyses revealed a weighted injury incidence of 17.8 (95% confidence interval [CI] 16.7-19.1) in novice runners and 7.7 (95% CI 6.9-8.7) in recreational runners.” (per 1,000 hours)Videbæk et al., Sports Med, 2015 (research)
Harmful before optimization

High-intensity interval training (HIIT)

What it gets right
Short, efficient sessions with real gains in fitness.
The HMR's view
HIIT combines high speed, high load and fatigue, the exact conditions in which an imperfect web fails. Fast, explosive force gives the tissue no time to share load; it hits the weakest line first.
“From 2012-2016 versus 2007-2011, there was a 144% increase in all injuries” “These increases in injury incidence correlated with a 274% increase in HIIT interest.”Rynecki et al., J Sports Med Phys Fitness, 2019 (research)
Harmful before optimization

CrossFit and high-intensity functional training

What it gets right
Community, variety and whole-body movement.
The HMR's view
Heavy Olympic lifts, gymnastics and speed in one session: the hardest loads, done tired, through a web that has never been tuned.
“A total of 132 responses were collected with 97 (73.5%) having sustained an injury during CrossFit training.” “An injury rate of 3.1 per 1000 hours trained was calculated.”Hak, Hodzovic and Hickey, J Strength Cond Res, 2013 (research)
“Current evidence suggests that the injury risk from CrossFit training is comparable to Olympic weightlifting, distance running, track and field, rugby, football, ice hockey, soccer, or gymnastics.”Klimek et al., J Sport Rehabil, 2018 (research)
Harmful before optimization

Most stretching routines

What it gets right
The instinct that tissue needs length and range.
The HMR's view
Short, forceful stretches pull hardest on whatever is already loose and barely reach what is stuck. Holds of 20 to 30 seconds also end before fascia has time to change. Stretching, as usually taught, does not prevent injury in the trials.
“Stretching was not significantly associated with a reduction in total injuries (OR = 0.93, CI 0.78-1.11)”Thacker et al. (CDC), Med Sci Sports Exerc, 2004 (research)
“Practical durations of passive stretching resulted in significant decreases in MTS; however, these changes return to baseline levels within 10 to 20 minutes.”Ryan et al., J Orthop Sports Phys Ther, 2008 (research; MTS is musculotendinous stiffness)
Harmful before optimization

Weight-loss programs

What it gets right
Excess weight does strain the body.
The HMR's view
Diets and calorie-burning exercise treat fat as the cause. In the HMR, where fat is stored is shaped by where the web is tight and where it is slack (see the densification hypothesis). Lose the weight without fixing the web, and the body tends to rebuild the same shape.
“After 6 years, 41.0 ± 31.3 kg of the lost weight was regained”Fothergill et al. (NIH), Obesity, 2016, follow-up of contestants who had lost 58.3 kg on average (research)
“Current perception is that participants of a structured weight-loss program regain all of their weight loss within 5 y.” In that meta-analysis, people kept off on average 17% to 29% of the weight they lost.Anderson et al., Am J Clin Nutr, 2001 (research)
Harmful before optimization

Team sports

What it gets right
Play, teamwork, joy and skill.
The HMR's view
Sudden cuts, tackles and sprints put peak force through the body with no warning, and the imperfect web cannot share it in time.
“Match injury incidence (36 injuries/1000 hours of exposure) was almost 10 times higher than training injury incidence rate (3.7 injuries/1000 hours of exposure).”López-Valenciano et al., Br J Sports Med, 2020, professional football (research)
Value with risks

Cycling

What it gets right
Low impact, good for the heart, easy on most joints.
The HMR's view
Hours in one folded posture, repeating one short arc of motion, shortens the front of the body and loads the lower back and knees the same way every time.
“Fifty-eight percent of all cyclists had experienced lower back pain in the previous 12 months” “Thirty-six percent had experienced anterior knee pain”Clarsen, Krosshaug and Bahr, Am J Sports Med, 2010, 109 professional road cyclists (research)
Value with risks

Swimming

What it gets right
Water supports the body and removes impact; it is one of the gentlest ways to move.
The HMR's view
Competitive training repeats one stroke tens of thousands of times, through shoulders that may not be ready for it, often in adolescents whose web is still forming.
“Adolescent swimmers showed the highest rates of shoulder pain (91.3%) compared with other age groups (range = 19.4%-70.3%).”Feijen et al., J Athl Train, 2020, systematic review (research)
Close to the HMR

Pilates

What it gets right
A lot. Slow, controlled movement, long holds, stretching under control and whole-body attention. It is pretty good exercise too.
The HMR's view
Pilates comes closest to working with the web instead of against it. Its limit is that it still treats the body as it is, rather than first defining what the optimized tensegrity should be.
“Two trials assessed adverse events in this review, one did not find any adverse events, and another reported minor events.”Yamato et al., Cochrane review of Pilates for low back pain, 2015 (research)
Close to the HMR

Yoga, especially long holds

What it gets right
Long-held poses, breath and attention: the traditions refined exactly the slow, sustained loading fascia responds to. Long hip-opening and yin-style sessions are close to the HMR's protocol.
The risk
Forcing range. Pushing into a pose the web is not ready for strains the same slack regions that every other program strains.
“There were 29,590 yoga-related injuries seen in hospital emergency departments from 2001 to 2014. The trunk (46.6%) was the most frequent region injured, and sprain/strain (45.0%) accounted for the majority of diagnoses.”Swain and McGwin, Orthop J Sports Med, 2016 (research)
“Beginners should avoid extreme practices such as headstand, lotus position and forceful breathing.”Cramer, Krucoff and Dobos, PLoS One, 2013 (research)
Close to the HMR

Tai chi and qigong

What it gets right
Slow, continuous, whole-body movement with breath, at low load. Among the gentlest forms of exercise studied.
The HMR's view
Excellent for balance and attention. On its own it rarely applies the long, deep holds that change dense fascia.
“TC is unlikely to result in serious AEs, but it may be associated with minor musculoskeletal aches and pains.”Wayne et al., Arch Phys Med Rehabil, 2014, review of 153 trials (research; TC is tai chi, AEs adverse events)

Evidence that cuts the other way

We report it plainly. Weight-training sports have lower injury rates than many team sports: “the weight-training sports appear to have relatively low rates of injury compared with common team sports” (Keogh and Winwood, Sports Med, 2017). In trials, “Strength training reduced sports injuries to less than 1/3 and overuse injuries could be almost halved” (Lauersen et al., Br J Sports Med, 2014).

The HMR does not dispute that movement and strength beat inactivity. Its claim is about order: the same training on an optimized tensegrity should do far more good, and far less harm. That is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

The HMR's order

Tensegrity first. Strength second.

  1. Define the optimized tensegrity. A physics model of the ideal human body, built from measurements, shows what each line of fascia should be. Physical Health →
  2. Mature the web toward it. Hydration, long holds, hands-on work and the inside-out practices that make fascia receptive to change (listed on Physical Health).
  3. Then train. Once force is shared evenly, strength, speed and endurance build on a sound structure instead of on its faults.

Done the other way around, exercise mostly reinforces the imperfections: the dense lines get denser and the slack ones weaker, and each year of training makes the faults harder to undo.

What it would feel like (the HMR's view)

Training an optimized tensegrity

  • No pain. Load spreads through the whole web, so no single joint or tendon takes the hit.
  • Much stronger. Every line contributes, so the whole body lifts, not a few overloaded muscles.
  • Lighter. A well-tensioned structure holds itself up; less effort goes into bracing against its own faults.
  • More capable. Range, balance and speed come from the structure itself, and gains stay when training pauses.

These are predictions of the HMR, to be tested in simulation and in people.

The solution: teach it, share it, start with children.

Fixing exercise is an education problem before it is a training problem. The HMR's plan: teach everyone what an optimized tensegrity is, develop open protocols that can be shared worldwide, and make fascia care as ordinary as brushing teeth.

The highest priority is children, before puberty and during it, when the web is still being built and is easiest to shape.

A hypothesis about puberty.

In the HMR, puberty is a signal that the growing tensegrity is reaching its bounds, and that the inner struts need to be aligned properly. If they are not, some parts grow faster than others, tension becomes uneven, and the tensegrity develops imperfections that adults then carry for life.

If that is right, the most valuable fascia care anyone ever receives is the care given to them as a child. (HYPOTHESIS: stated as such, to be tested.)
